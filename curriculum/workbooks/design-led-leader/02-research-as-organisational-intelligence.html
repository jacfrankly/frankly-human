Opening story · draft

Priya's team shipped twelve
studies last year. Zero reached the C-suite.

  • Head of Design Research at Solstice Health, a mid-size digital health company
  • Her team ran rigorous usability studies — every one improved a screen, a flow, a conversion number
  • Every quarter, leadership set strategy in a room her research never entered
  • Then a routine onboarding study surfaced something bigger: patients weren't dropping off because of the form — they were dropping off because the product assumed a diagnosis they hadn't received yet

The finding didn't change. Priya did — she stopped pitching it as a screen fix and started pitching it as a bet: build for the undiagnosed, not just the diagnosed. That one made the strategy deck.

Two kinds of rigor, aimed differently

Tactical

  • Answers "does this screen work"
  • Judged in a design review
  • Measured in conversion, task success, error rate
  • Valuable — and invisible above the design org

Strategic

  • Answers "is this the right bet"
  • Judged in a strategy conversation
  • Measured in market position, risk, resourcing
  • Same discipline, aimed at a different decision

The same discipline that improves a feature can reposition a function — if it's aimed at the decisions executives are already trying to make.

Redirect toward the decisions already on the table

Strategic research doesn't
invent the question. It finds it.

  • A market or segment bet they haven't said out loud yet
  • A build-vs-buy or build-vs-partner call under time pressure
  • A resourcing trade-off between two priorities that both sound urgent
  • A risk they're carrying that hasn't been named in the room

Redirecting a research function toward strategic questions starts with knowing what those questions actually are — not guessing, asking.

Same finding, different room

Design review packaging

  • Screens, flows, before/after
  • Usability metrics: task success, time on task
  • Audience: designers, PMs
  • Question answered: "did we build it right?"

Strategy conversation packaging

  • One slide, one bet, one number
  • Framed as risk, market, or resourcing stakes
  • Audience: executives who don't have time for the screens
  • Question answered: "should we build this at all?"

"Package findings for a strategy conversation, not a design review" — the content doesn't just get compressed, it gets re-aimed.

Activity 01 · 20 min · Draft

Reframe a Finding

Activity 0120 min

Take one real tactical finding from your team's last quarter and rewrite it as a one-slide strategic narrative — pitched at an executive, not a design review.

  1. Individual — write the finding as your team currently reports it (the tactical version)
  2. Rewrite it as a bet: what decision does it inform, what's the stakes, what number does it move
  3. Cut all UI language — no "usability," "friction," "flow," "intuitive"
  4. Pairs — pitch the reframed version aloud in 60 seconds, as if to your CEO
Activity 01 (20 min) — DRAFT: Watch for reframes that keep design language dressed up in exec syntax ("improve the experience" → "de-risk retention") — press for an actual number or named risk. This activity is invented to fit the framework; not sourced from the docx.
One good pitch doesn't build trust. A pattern does.

Executive trust is a ledger,
not a moment.

  • One great strategic pitch gets you in the room once
  • Trust compounds from a pattern of research landing on the right desk, repeatedly
  • Each well-aimed deposit makes the next conversation easier to get
  • Untargeted research — even excellent research — makes no deposit at all

Building the habit of research as an executive-trust-building activity means treating every finding as a potential deposit — and asking, before you ship it, whose ledger it belongs in.

Activity 02 · 20 min · Draft

The Trust Ledger

Activity 0220 min

Identify one executive relationship where research could build trust — and name the specific strategic question that executive is actually wrestling with right now.

  1. Name one executive relationship you want to strengthen this quarter
  2. Name the specific strategic question they're wrestling with — not a guess dressed as fact; if you're not sure, name how you'd check
  3. Name one piece of research — existing or plannable — that speaks directly to that question
  4. Pairs — pressure-test: would this actually land on their desk, or is it still shaped for a design review?
Activity 02 (20 min) — DRAFT: Watch for vague "strategic questions" that are really just guesses — push for how they'd confirm it (a real conversation, not an assumption). This activity is invented to fit the framework; not sourced from the docx.
