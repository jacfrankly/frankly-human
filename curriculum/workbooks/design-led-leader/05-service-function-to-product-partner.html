Opening story · draft

Elena's tickets kept arriving
pre-scoped.

  • Six months after the CFO paragraph landed, Elena finally got budget language people listened to
  • But the tickets on her team's board still showed up with scope, timeline, and success metric already fixed
  • The reason: a recurring Quarterly Planning Sync, run by the PM lead and eng director — design isn't on the invite
  • By the time a ticket reaches design, the only open question left is "how," never "whether" or "why"

Credibility got her heard. It didn't get her into the room where the call actually gets made.

Where the trap actually happens

The trap has
an address.

  • A planning sync design isn't invited to, where scope and timeline get fixed
  • A ticket template with effort and success-metric fields already filled in before design opens it
  • An approval gate that only requires product and engineering sign-off to greenlight
  • A roadmap review where "design implications" is item six of six, if it's an item at all

"The Service Department Trap" isn't a feeling. It's a location — a specific point in a specific process. Find the address, and you've found the lever.

Concept · core claim
"

Escaping the Service Department Trap requires changing where in the process design enters — not working harder once it's already too late to shape the outcome.

No amount of extra polish on a ticket fixes a decision that was already made before design saw it. Working harder downstream doesn't move the entry point. Only a structural change does.

Two different kinds of "better"

One-off fix

  • Getting looped in on this one initiative
  • A favor from a sympathetic PM
  • Solves this quarter, not next
  • Depends entirely on who's in the room

Durable structural change

  • Design has a standing seat at the planning sync itself
  • Written into the process, not a person's goodwill
  • Survives a reorg or a PM leaving
  • Changes the default, not just this instance

Elena's next move can't just be "get looped in on this initiative." It has to target the sync itself.

A seat at the sync
isn't the finish line.

  • Getting into the room only matters if you can make the actual call once you're there
  • A product partner doesn't just comment on someone else's sequencing — they can produce one
  • That means holding capacity, dependency, and commercial tradeoffs at once — not just design judgment
  • Next is that call, for real: one team, three competing initiatives, a defensible order

Activity 02 · 30 min · Draft · The Real Rep

The Sequencing Call

One team. Three competing initiatives. Not enough capacity for all three this quarter. This is the actual call a product partner has to make — not a simulation of it.

  • Initiative A — Checkout Redesign. 8 eng-weeks / 3 design-weeks. Blocked on the Platform team's payment API, ready in ~5 weeks. Modeled upside: +$1.2M ARR from reduced cart abandonment. A competitor shipped one-click checkout last month.
  • Initiative B — Self-Serve Onboarding. 10 eng-weeks / 4 design-weeks. No dependency, ready to start now. Opens a new SMB segment worth ~$400K ARR over 12 months, and unblocks Marketing's stalled paid-acquisition motion.
  • Initiative C — Enterprise Dashboard Rebuild. 6 eng-weeks / 2 design-weeks. No dependency. Flagged by the top 5 enterprise accounts ($2.1M ARR combined); one $450K account renews in 7 weeks and is explicitly at risk of churn.

Team capacity this quarter: 18 engineering-weeks, 6 design-weeks. The ask across all three: 24 engineering-weeks, 9 design-weeks. You cannot fully resource all three.

Activity 0230 min

Produce a sequencing decision — first, second, third — and defend each placement in commercial and capacity terms, not craft terms.

  1. Individual — rank the three initiatives 1st, 2nd, 3rd. No ties
  2. For each, write one sentence justifying the position using the numbers above — capacity, dependency, or commercial risk — not "it feels more important"
  3. Name what you'd say to the stakeholder whose initiative lands third — the one sentence that doesn't dodge the tradeoff
  4. Pairs — trade sequencing decisions and argue for the opposite ranking for 5 minutes. See if your original order survives
Activity 02 (30 min) — DRAFT, THE REAL REP: Give this the most weight and time of the module. There is a defensible answer (C first — hard 7-week deadline, small effort, protects existing revenue; A second, using the API-blocked weeks for design/discovery in parallel; B deferred — largest ask, no hard deadline) but the point is the reasoning, not matching a key. Push back hard on any ranking justified by design preference rather than the numbers given. Scenario and figures are invented to fit the framework; not sourced from the docx.
Synthesis · 10 min

The trap is structural. So is the way out.

Naming the exact handoff point is the first structural move — you can't redesign a meeting you can't name.
A durable fix changes the default. A one-off fix just changes this ticket.
Sequencing three initiatives under real constraints is the rep that earns the seat — not the pitch for why design deserves one.
