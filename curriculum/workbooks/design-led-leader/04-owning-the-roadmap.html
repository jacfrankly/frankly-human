Owning the Roadmap

Reacting to the roadmap and setting it are two different jobs.
Roadmap influence isn't granted by title — it's earned by showing up early enough, with enough commercial fluency, to shape the question before it's already been answered.

Opening story · draft

Priya got the roadmap deck
three days before lock.

  • Head of Design at Vantage Health, a mid-size health-tech scale-up
  • Q1: the roadmap arrived as a Slack link two days before the all-hands — budget and headcount already signed off
  • Her only live decision left was which screens to make beautiful, not which problems were worth solving
  • Q3, same company: she asked onto the pre-read circulation eight weeks before lock, sent a one-page point of view unprompted, and one of her sequencing calls became the quarter's actual plan

Same person, same company, two quarters apart. The difference wasn't her ability — it was when she showed up.

Every roadmap has a cycle — most design leaders only see the end of it

Reacting to the roadmap
and setting it
are two different jobs.

  • Signal-gathering — problems, data and pressures surface informally, weeks before anything is written down
  • Pre-read circulation — a shortlist of bets gets shared with a small circle for reaction, before it's a deck
  • Roadmap lock — priorities, budget and headcount get signed off; this is usually where design finds out
  • Comms — the locked roadmap gets announced to the wider org as a fait accompli

The bet nobody asked you to make yet

What's asked of you

  • Build a point of view on the roadmap before being asked for one
  • Practise defending a strategic bet in commercial, not craft, language

One way to structure it · draft scaffold

  • One sentence: the bet you'd make if it were your call
  • The commercial case for it — a number, a risk, or a cost, not a craft argument
  • What you'd trade off to fund it — nothing is free; name what waits
  • Whose data or judgment this leans on

Relationship Capital, applied to one bet

Identify whose backing you need
before you propose it, not after.

  • Sponsor — the person whose name on it changes whether it's taken seriously
  • Pressure-tester — the peer who will find the hole in it before a room does, safely
  • Informed-late — who needs to hear it from you before they hear it from someone else

Relationship Capital doesn't replace a good business case. In a political, relationship-oriented organisation, it decides whether the good business case gets heard at all.

Before the activity — the facts you're working with

The Scarcity Call

The constraint: You have 2 senior designers for one quarter — 2.0 designer-quarters of capacity. These three initiatives, combined, need 5.0 designer-quarters. Something doesn't fit.
A · Enterprise SSO & Permissions Rebuild2.0 designer-quarters

Two enterprise deals worth $340K combined ARR are contractually conditional on SSO and role-based permissions shipping this quarter. Sales has already given the client a date. The CRO has flagged the risk in the last two forecast calls.

B · Onboarding Redesign — Activation Lift1.5 designer-quarters

Trial-to-paid conversion sits at 11%. A partial A/B test on the signup flow already showed a lift to ~15% on the tested segment — modeled at roughly $180K incremental ARR per quarter at current trial volume if the full lift holds. No hard deadline, but the opportunity cost compounds as trial volume grows.

C · Self-Serve Analytics Dashboard1.5 designer-quarters

Cited as a top-3 lost-deal reason by Sales in 4 of the last 6 lost enterprise evaluations. A competitive-parity ask — a defensive bet, not yet a proven revenue driver.

Activity 02 · Part 1 of 2 · 20 min · Draft — The Real Rep

The Scarcity Call

Activity 02 · Part 120 min

Score all three initiatives against the facts on the previous slide, then choose which ship this quarter and which wait — the constraint doesn't move.

  1. Score each initiative's Value 1–10 (commercial impact, strategic risk, urgency) using the facts given, not vibes
  2. Divide Value by Effort (designer-quarters) to get a rough priority ratio for each
  3. Decide your sequence — you have 2.0 designer-quarters, the three together need 5.0. At least one initiative waits, in part or in full, this quarter
Activity 02, Part 1 (20 min) — DRAFT: Push back hard on any sequence that tries to do all three "a little bit each" — that's refusing the constraint, not solving it. 2.0 designer-quarters, period. This is a real rep: treat the number as load-bearing, not a suggestion.
Activity 02 · Part 2 of 2 · 15 min · Draft — The Real Rep

Defend the Sequence

Activity 02 · Part 215 min

Write the one paragraph you'd actually say out loud to defend your sequence — in commercial language, naming whose backing you'd need before proposing it.

  1. No craft language — explain the sequence the way a CRO or CFO would want it explained
  2. Name specifically why the initiative(s) that wait, wait — not just why the chosen one won
  3. Name whose backing you'd need before taking this sequence to the room
  4. Pairs — read it aloud; if your partner can poke a hole in 30 seconds, tighten it before moving on
Activity 02, Part 2 (15 min) — DRAFT: The tell that a defence is weak: it explains why the winner is good without explaining why the other two can survive waiting. Both halves are required, every time — never let "we'll just do all three" pass as an answer.
Synthesis · 10 min

Roadmap influence is earned before the meeting, not argued for inside it.

Showing up early enough matters more than arguing harder once the roadmap is already locked.
A defensible sequence names why the other two wait — not just why the one you chose is good.
One point of view, three initiatives, one scarce quarter: this is the rep, not a rehearsal for one.
