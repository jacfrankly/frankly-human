Executive Decision Hygiene

The same decision hygiene that improves a product call improves a board call —

Opening story · draft

Named criteria. A pre-mortem.
Clean analysis. They still lost.

  • A leadership team facing a genuine build-vs-partner call, evaluated against five weighted criteria agreed in advance
  • A pre-mortem surfaced the two ways it could fail — both were priced into the recommendation
  • The analysis was clean, defensible, and unanimous among the people who ran the process
  • The CEO's preferred option — backed by the newest, most senior exec in the room — won anyway

Good process doesn't guarantee the win. It changes what you can do about the loss — and whether you saw it coming.

Story (5 min) — DRAFT: This is a placeholder composite, not a named real client. If Jacinta has a real, anonymisable case with this shape, swap it in — it will land harder than an invented one.
The same hygiene, a higher-stakes table

The tools don't change.
The stakes do.

  • Named criteria, agreed before anyone knows which option they'll favour
  • A pre-mortem — naming how this fails before it ships, not after
  • Decoupling the decision from who's most senior in the room
  • None of this is exotic. Most functioning product teams already do it at project level

Good judgment at board level follows the same hygiene as good judgment at project level — named criteria, pre-mortems, decoupling the decision from who's most senior in the room.

04
Concept (8 min): Land on "the tools don't change, the politics do" — it sets up the honest gap on the next slide. Source: Design_Led_Leader_Curriculum.docx.
The honest gap

Hygiene improves the call.
It doesn't guarantee the win.

  • In a rational room, the best-supported option wins
  • In a political room — most rooms — sponsorship, history, and seniority also vote
  • Decision hygiene doesn't make a room rational. It makes the call defensible regardless of what the room does
  • This module doesn't pretend that gap away. It teaches you to see it before the room votes

In a political organisation, decision hygiene improves the quality of the call without guaranteeing it wins. That gap is exactly where this module — and Relationship Capital — meet.

Quick hit · 5 min
"

Name a good decision you made
that led to a bad outcome.

How do you know the difference — and did you know it at the time?

Quick Hit 01 (5 min): Collect 3–4 answers from the room. Watch for people who can't separate the two yet — that's exactly what the next concept slide addresses.
Two different judgments

A good decision can lose.
A bad decision can win.

  • A good decision is judged by the process and the information available at the time it was made
  • A good outcome is judged by what happened afterward — often shaped by luck, timing, or forces outside the room
  • Conflating the two teaches leaders the wrong lesson from both wins and losses
  • Separate them on purpose, especially in the debrief after a call goes badly

If you can only defend a decision when it works out, you didn't actually have a decision process. You had a guess with good PR.

Activity 01 · 15 min · Draft

Build the Decision Matrix

Activity 0115 min

Apply a simple weighted-criteria decision matrix to one real, current leadership-level choice you're facing.

  1. Individual — name the decision and 3–5 criteria that actually matter, not the ones that sound good in a deck
  2. Weight each criterion out of 10, then score each option against every criterion
  3. Pairs — trade matrices and stress-test each other's weighting — 5 minutes
Activity 01 (15 min) — DRAFT: Watch for criteria that are really proxies for a favoured option ("speed to market" when they mean "the option I already like"). This activity is invented to fit the framework; not sourced from the docx.
Two different games

Two different games.
Play the right one.

  • Some decisions are won on the merits — the best-supported option, argued well, carries the room
  • Some are won on sponsorship — whoever has the most senior, most invested backer carries the room instead
  • Misreading which game you're in wastes a rigorous case on a room that was never going to be moved by rigor
  • Reading it early tells you whether to keep refining the analysis or start building the relationship

The room in the opening story ran a merits process in a sponsorship room. That's the mistake to catch before it costs you the call.

Is your current decision heading for

Notice which answer you're more comfortable admitting.

Activity 02 · 15 min · Draft

Merits or Sponsorship?

Activity 0215 min

For the same decision from Activity 01, honestly assess whether it will actually be won on the merits or on sponsorship — and if sponsorship, name whose backing you need and how you'll get it.

  1. Individual — merits or sponsorship, honestly, not the answer that sounds better
  2. If sponsorship — name the specific person whose backing actually moves this room
  3. Pairs — name one concrete step to build that relationship capital before the decision is made
Activity 02 (15 min) — DRAFT: Watch for participants who name sponsorship but then propose only more analysis as the "plan" — that's still playing the merits game. Push for a relational step. This activity is invented to fit the framework; not sourced from the docx.
Synthesis · 10 min

Decision hygiene earns you a defensible call. It doesn't buy you the room.

Named criteria and a pre-mortem make the call better, whether or not it wins.
A good decision and a good outcome are two different judgments — keep them separate.
When the room runs on sponsorship, the relationship is the process.
Synthesis (10 min): This is the bridge into Module 13 — decision hygiene and relationship capital aren't competing skills, they're the same leader reading the room correctly.
