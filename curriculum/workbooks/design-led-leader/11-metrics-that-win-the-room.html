Opening story · draft

One dashboard is beautiful.
The other gets read.

  • A design team built a polished internal dashboard — NPS, task-completion rate, design-system adoption
  • Refreshed monthly, presented proudly at the design all-hands
  • Nobody outside design has ever opened it
  • Meanwhile, a business dashboard already gets a weekly executive glance — revenue, retention, activation, cost to serve
  • Design's impact is nowhere on it — not because it isn't real, but because no one built the bridge

The team wasn't wrong about their impact. They were wrong about which dashboard it needed to live on.

The core claim

Executive trust compounds
where executives already look.

  • Executive trust compounds when a function's impact is visible in the metrics leadership already watches
  • Not in a separate design-only scorecard nobody else reads
  • A design-only dashboard proves design cares about design. It doesn't prove design moves the business
  • The bar isn't "do we have good metrics" — it's "are we inside the metrics someone else already trusts"
04
Concept (8 min): The core claim, verbatim. Source: Design_Led_Leader_Curriculum.docx. This is the spine the rest of the module builds on — land it before moving to vanity vs. driver metrics.
Some metrics feel good. Some earn credibility.

Vanity metric

  • Feels good to report
  • Has rarely, if ever, changed a decision
  • Moves independent of anything the business already cares about
  • Safe to report because it never gets challenged

Driver metric

  • Tied to a business outcome someone else already tracks
  • Moving it changes what leadership does next
  • Invites scrutiny — precisely because it matters
  • Earns the function credibility, not just applause

A vanity metric is comfortable exactly because it's disconnected. A driver metric is uncomfortable exactly because it isn't.

One outcome. A few drivers. Every decision traces back.

A metric tree is a chain
of custody.

  • Top: the one business outcome that matters most for a product area — revenue, retention, activation — whatever the business already tracks
  • Middle: the 2–3 driver metrics that actually move the top-line outcome — the load-bearing few, not everything measurable
  • Bottom: the specific decisions — including design decisions — that move a driver
  • A tree isn't a dashboard. It's a traceable chain from a UI decision to a number the CFO already watches

Activity 01 · 15 min · Draft

Kill a Vanity Metric

Activity 0115 min

List 2–3 metrics your team currently tracks. Honestly sort them: which one is vanity, which one earns real credibility.

  1. Individual — list 2–3 metrics your team reports regularly
  2. For each, ask: has this number ever changed a decision, in either direction?
  3. Circle the one that's vanity — feels good, changes nothing
  4. Pairs — defend your circle, or get talked out of it — 5 minutes
Activity 01 (15 min) — DRAFT: Watch for participants defending a vanity metric because it's the one they personally built or championed — the discomfort of circling it is the point, not a bug in the exercise. This activity is invented; not sourced from the docx.
Design decisions don't sit at the top

Design decisions sit
underneath the driver.

  • Design rarely owns the top-line outcome — it owns a lever that moves a driver
  • Onboarding flow → activation rate → revenue
  • Search relevance → task success → retention
  • Design-system consistency → support ticket volume → cost to serve
  • The job isn't claiming credit for the top line. It's making the chain visible enough that someone else can see it too

Activity 02 · 30 min total · Draft · The Real Rep

Build the North Star Tree — Part 1

This is the module's real rep. Not a reflection prompt — an actual worked metric tree for a product area you lead, built the way a product manager would be expected to build one.

Activity 02 · Part 115 min

Choose one real product area you lead. Name the single top-line business outcome it should move — a number that already exists on a business dashboard.

  1. Individual — name the product area
  2. Name the top-line business outcome — the number leadership already watches
  3. List the 2–3 driver metrics that most directly move that outcome — the load-bearing few
  4. For each driver, ask: if this number moved 10%, would anyone outside design notice, and would it matter to them?
Activity 02, Part 1 (15 min) — DRAFT, REAL REP: Per source doc, this is one of the four "real reps" modules — the goal is a leader who can't be out-judged by a product manager because they've done a version of the PM's actual job. Push for a real, current product area, not a hypothetical. Never let a "driver metric" actually be a vanity metric relabeled — press once on whether it truly moves the top-line outcome.
Activity 02 · 30 min total · Draft · The Real Rep

Build the North Star Tree — Part 2

Activity 02 · Part 215 min

For your strongest driver, name one specific design decision — already made, or still open — that would move it. Then stress-test the whole chain.

  1. Individual — name the specific design decision that moves your strongest driver
  2. Write the full chain in one line: decision → driver → outcome
  3. Stress-test: would a CFO recognise this as a real chain, not a design story dressed up in business language?
  4. Pairs — trade trees, try to find the weakest link in your partner's chain — 10 minutes
Activity 02, Part 2 (15 min) — DRAFT, REAL REP: This is the part most likely to expose a relabeled vanity metric — if the "design decision" is vague ("we made it easier to use") press for the specific screen, flow, or default that changed. The pairs stress-test is not optional; a chain that's never been challenged hasn't earned the name.
Synthesis · 10 min

The dashboard doesn't need you in the room. It needs to already be trusted before you walk in.

A vanity metric is comfortable exactly because it's disconnected from any decision.
A North Star tree is uncomfortable exactly because it makes the chain — and its weak links — visible.
Executive trust isn't a separate scorecard. It's showing up inside the one that already exists.
