Eleven weeks. Four meetings.
Nobody disagreed.

  • Head of Design at Halyard, a mid-size B2B SaaS company
  • The question on the table: which of three products gets next quarter's engineering headcount
  • Three product leads, two VPs, eleven weeks, four meetings that each re-opened the same question
  • Nobody's argument actually changed between meeting one and meeting four

The headcount call wasn't hard. Knowing who was allowed to make it was.

The core claim

Decisions have
structure too.

  • Information architecture is the discipline of structuring content so people can find things without thinking about the structure itself
  • A confusing nav doesn't fail because people are dumb — it fails because nobody designed the paths
  • A confusing decision process doesn't fail because people disagree — it fails because nobody designed who decides, in what order, with what inputs
  • The practitioner skill and the leadership skill are the same skill, aimed at a different artefact

The same discipline that untangles a confusing navigation can untangle a confusing decision-making process — and whoever untangles it earns quiet authority over it.

Concept (8 min): Land hard on the practitioner-to-leader translation — this is the meta-pattern for the whole module (and the reason it maps to Information Architecture). Source: Design_Led_Leader_Curriculum.docx, Module 08 outline.
Diagram the decision, not the org chart

Every decision has two
maps. Most leaders only see one.

  • The formal path: the org chart, the approval chain, the steps in the process doc
  • The informal path: who actually has to nod first, who gets looped in out of habit, who gets skipped even though they're "supposed" to sign off
  • The informal path is the real one — and it's rarely written down anywhere
  • Diagramming both, side by side, is the first move: name where they diverge

Leaders leave this module able to diagram how a real organisational decision actually gets made, including the informal parts.

Quick hit · 5 min
"

Think of one decision that took far
longer than it should have. What actually
made it slow?

Not the topic — the mechanism. Was it unclear ownership, a missing sign-off, a loop nobody could close?

Quick Hit 01 (5 min): Collect 3–4 answers from the room. Expect "unclear ownership" to dominate — note the pattern for Activity 01.
The tell

"It's fine, it's just Dave"
is rarely the whole story.

  • If the same kind of decision stalls with different people in the seat, it's structural
  • If the structure would work fine except one specific person keeps blocking it, look again before you conclude that
  • A person who "always has to weigh in" might be doing that because the structure never gave them a formal role — so they built an informal one
  • Removing the person without fixing the structure just creates a vacancy for the next Dave

Leaders leave this module able to identify where a decision structure is the actual blocker, not the people in it.

Concept (7 min): The italicised line is a direct source quote (outcome 2 of 3). This is the trap to watch for all module: personalising a structural failure. If someone says "it's just Dave," ask what would happen if Dave left tomorrow — usually the same bottleneck reappears with someone else's name on it.
Activity 01 · 20 min · Draft

Diagram a Real Decision

Activity 0120 min

Pick one recent, real, slow organisational decision. Diagram how it actually got made.

  1. Individual — list every step the decision formally went through (the process-doc version)
  2. Add the informal steps: who really had to sign off, who got looped in out of habit, where it looped back
  3. Circle the one point where it stalled longest
  4. Pairs — compare diagrams, 5 minutes — what surprised you about the other person's decision?
Activity 01 (20 min) — DRAFT: Watch for participants picking a decision that's still too fresh or too politically live to diagram honestly — nudge toward something resolved, even messily, in the last quarter. This activity is invented to fit the framework; not sourced from the docx.
How the room hears it

The same proposal lands two
different ways. The frame decides which.

  • "I think I should decide this" reads as a power grab, even when it's true
  • "Here's where the decision keeps stalling, and here's a structure that would stop it stalling" reads as service
  • Naming the mechanism, not the outcome you want, is what makes the difference
  • The sentence that introduces the proposal matters as much as the proposal itself

Leaders leave this module able to propose a cleaner decision structure without it reading as a power grab.

Concept (7 min): The italicised line is a direct source quote (outcome 3 of 3). This is the pivot into Activity 02 — the "introduction sentence" is the actual skill being built.
Quick hit · 5 min
"

If you proposed fixing your slowest
decision tomorrow, what would people
assume your motive was?

Naming the assumption is the first step to writing around it.

Quick Hit 02 (5 min): Take 3–4 answers from the room. Note whether the assumed motive is about turf or about control — it previews how defensively Activity 02 will need to be framed for this cohort.
Activity 02 · 20 min · Draft

Propose the Cleaner Structure

Activity 0220 min

For the same decision, propose a cleaner decision structure — and write the one sentence you'd use to introduce it.

  1. Sketch the cleaner structure — who decides, who's consulted, who's informed, in what order
  2. Name the mechanism it fixes, not just the outcome you want
  3. Write the exact opening sentence you'd use to introduce this to the room
  4. Pairs — read the sentence aloud — does it sound like service or like a grab? Revise once.
Activity 02 (20 min) — DRAFT: Watch for the introduction sentence still centring "I" rather than the mechanism — that's the tell it'll read as a grab. This activity is invented to fit the framework; not sourced from the docx.
Synthesis · 10 min

The business decides the way it's structured to decide — not the way anyone intends.

A decision that stalls isn't proof the people are wrong — it's data about the structure.
Diagramming the informal path is the practitioner skill. Proposing a fix without triggering defensiveness is the leadership skill.
Whoever untangles the structure quietly earns the authority to keep untangling it.
