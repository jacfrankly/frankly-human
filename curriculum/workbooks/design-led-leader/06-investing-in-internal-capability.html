Opening story · draft

Priya asked for a research ops hire
three times. Declined three times.

  • Head of Design at a mid-size logistics SaaS company, team of 11 measured almost entirely on tickets closed and sprint throughput
  • Pitched a research ops hire as "this will make our research better" — declined, no number attached
  • Pitched a design system tooling licence as "it'll make the team faster and happier" — declined, filed under nice-to-have
  • Finance never said no to research, or to tooling. They said no to a pitch with no payback period, no risk framing — nothing to compare against any other line on the sheet

The third time, she stopped pitching craft and started pitching a spreadsheet. It was funded in eleven days.

The trap this module breaks

When velocity is the only metric
in the room, investment dies.

  • The Feature Factory Manager — measured on velocity and output, not outcomes or strategy (one of the three traps, Module 01)
  • Velocity metrics answer "are we shipping fast" — never "are we shipping the right thing, and what's it costing us not to invest"
  • Every velocity metric crowds out a strategic conversation it never has to justify not having
  • Recognising the trap is the first move. Naming which conversation it's replacing is the second

Speaking finance's language

The maths behind the pitch —
not just the pitch.

  • Payback period — how long until the investment's savings repay its cost (Cost ÷ Net monthly benefit)
  • Risk-adjusted return — the same maths, discounted by how confident you actually are in the savings estimate
  • Capacity unlocked — what the freed-up time or reduced risk lets the team do next, priced at its real cost basis
  • None of this requires a finance degree. It requires doing the division, and being honest about the discount

The payback case, worked

One tool. Ten designers.
A payback period you can defend.

Investment — tooling licence (annual) + one-time implementation$24,000 + $12,000 = $36,000
Team affected — product designers, blended fully-loaded cost10 designers × $85/hr
Time saved — no longer hand-rebuilding components3 hrs / week / designer
Monthly time saved (3 × 10 × 4.33 weeks)≈ 130 hours / month
Monthly $ saved (130 hrs × $85/hr)$11,050 / month
Monthly net benefit (savings − ongoing $2,000/mo licence)$9,050 / month
Simple payback period = $36,000 ÷ $9,050/month ≈ 4 months.

Risk-adjusted: assume the tool only delivers 50% of the estimated time savings — a conservative confidence factor for a first-year estimate. Net monthly benefit drops to $3,525. Payback period stretches to just over 10 months — and the case still holds.

Activity 02 · 25 min · Draft · The real rep

Calculate Your Own Payback Case

Activity 0225 min

Take a real (or realistic) investment ask — a tool, a hire, a capability — and calculate its actual payback period and risk-adjusted return. Same maths as the worked example. Your numbers.

  1. Name the ask and its total Year 1 cost (licence or salary + implementation, all-in)
  2. Name who it affects, how many people, and their blended hourly cost basis
  3. Estimate hours saved (or capacity unlocked) per week per person, and calculate the monthly $ benefit
  4. Calculate simple payback period: total cost ÷ net monthly benefit
  5. Apply an honest confidence factor (50–70% is normal for a first estimate) and recalculate the risk-adjusted payback period
  6. Pairs — pressure-test each other's confidence factor: is it earned, or optimism?
Activity 02 (25 min) — DRAFT — the real rep: This is the module's centre of gravity. Don't let anyone skip to the pitch — make them do the division out loud. Circulate and check arithmetic once per table if they share numbers, but never do the maths for them. If someone says "it'll obviously pay for itself," that's the moment to ask for the actual number — don't let the line stand unchecked.
Synthesis · 10 min

Budget authority isn't a better pitch. It's better maths, held with confidence.

A payback period a CFO can check themselves is worth more than a story only design finds moving.
Risk-adjusted honesty builds more trust than an inflated number that gets caught out later.
The Feature Factory Manager trap doesn't go away because you named it once — it goes away when your asks keep surviving the maths.
