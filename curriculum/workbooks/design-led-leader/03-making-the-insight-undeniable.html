Opening story · draft

Priya's churn analysis was right.
It died on slide 4.

  • Head of Design at Solstice Health, a mid-size health-tech scale-up
  • Her team's churn analysis was rigorous, correct, and nailed the real driver of drop-off
  • It shipped as a 40-slide deck. Leadership skimmed the summary slide, nodded, moved on
  • Nothing changed. Nobody in that room had fifteen minutes and no other priorities that week
  • Two months later, a smaller finding — restructured onto one page — was read before the meeting even started, and reset the quarter's roadmap

The first analysis was right. The second one was undeniable.

The core claim

Being right isn't the job.
Being actionable is.

  • Analysis isn't leadership currency by default — it has to be structured to become it
  • It becomes currency only when it's shaped as a narrative someone with fifteen minutes and five other priorities can act on
  • That's a communication muscle, not a rigor problem — Priya's analysis was already rigorous
  • The Silent Expert trap isn't about not knowing the answer. It's about never making the answer easy to act on
04
Concept (8 min): The tagline and core claim are sourced verbatim from the module brief. Land the distinction hard: this isn't a call to be less rigorous — it's a second, separate skill most analytical leaders never trained.
Four moves, one page
  • Finding — the single most important thing you learned, stated as a claim, not a topic
  • So what — why it matters to a decision this room is already making
  • Recommendation — the specific action you want taken, not a menu of options
  • Risk of inaction — what it costs to do nothing, in terms the room already tracks

A theme is not a finding. "Onboarding needs work" is a theme. "Users who don't complete step 3 within 48 hours churn at 3x the rate" is a finding.

Objections are predictable
  • Every room raises roughly the same three kinds of objections — methodology, cost or feasibility, and "we tried this before"
  • Naming them before they're asked reframes the conversation from defence to decision
  • A one-line answer prepared in advance reads as command of the material, not defensiveness
  • Silence on an obvious objection reads as either not having thought of it, or hoping nobody notices

Activity 01 · 20 min · Draft

Build The One-Pager

Activity 0120 min

Take a real piece of analysis from your own work and compress it into a board-ready one-page narrative.

  1. Individual — pick one real, current piece of analysis from your own function
  2. Write the finding as a claim, not a topic — one sentence, no hedging
  3. Write the so-what, the recommendation, and the risk of inaction — one line each
  4. Pairs — read it aloud in under 60 seconds. If it takes longer, cut
Activity 01 (20 min) — DRAFT: Never write the one-pager for them. Watch for a "finding" that stays a vague theme rather than a specific, actionable statement — press once: "what's the number, the claim, the sentence?"
Knowing when to bet
  • Not every finding deserves a recommendation attached to it — some are directional, not decision-ready
  • A finding is strong enough to bet on when the cost of being wrong is smaller than the cost of staying silent
  • Sample size, replication, and how much the finding surprised you all matter more than how confident you feel saying it
  • Leaders who are right in private and silent in public aren't protecting their credibility — they're spending it on nothing

Quick hit · 5 min
"

What's the objection you always
brace for — do you have a one-line answer yet?

If the honest answer is no, that's the useful part.

Quick Hit 02 (5 min): Take 3–4 answers from the room. Note whether the objection is genuinely about the work or about the person raising it — that distinction matters for Activity 02.
Activity 02 · 15 min · Draft

Pre-Empt the Room

Activity 0215 min

For the same recommendation, name the three objections a specific real room would raise — and draft a one-line answer to each before you're asked.

  1. Individual — name the specific room: which meeting, which people, receiving this recommendation
  2. List the three objections that room, specifically, would raise
  3. Draft a one-line answer to each — a line, not a paragraph
  4. Pairs — swap and stress-test: would that answer actually survive being asked live?
Activity 02 (15 min) — DRAFT: Never draft the objections or answers for them. Watch for objections that are actually about a named person, not the work — redirect to the structural pattern the room will raise regardless of who's in the seat.
Synthesis · 10 min

An insight that doesn't change a decision wasn't really an insight.

Rigor gets you a correct finding. Structure gets you a heard one.
The room's objections aren't a threat to brace for — they're the map of what to answer first.
The Silent Expert trap doesn't break by being more right. It breaks by being more readable.
Synthesis (10 min): This is the bridge into Module 04 — a one-pager that survives the room's objections is the raw material for owning the roadmap, not just defending a single recommendation.
