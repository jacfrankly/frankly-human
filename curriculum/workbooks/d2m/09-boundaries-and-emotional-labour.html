Emotional labour

If it's invisible,
it's unsustainable.

Emotional labour is the work of managing feelings — yours and everyone else's — so a room, a relationship, or a family keeps running smoothly. It rarely gets named, which is exactly why it's exhausting.

  • Managing others' feelings
  • Anticipating needs before they're spoken
  • Keeping the peace
  • Being "the strong one"
  • Being the organiser

Boundary types

Different boundaries
solve different problems.

Boundaries aren't walls — they're design constraints. Naming which kind you need makes it easier to actually hold.

  • Time — how long, how often, when
  • Energy — what depletes you and what restores you
  • Emotional — what you'll carry, and what isn't yours to carry
  • Communication — how and when people can reach you
  • Physical — your body, your space, your touch
  • Digital — your attention, your notifications, your inbox
05
Concept (10 min): Read the six types plainly, don't over-explain. Ask: "Which type do you break most often?" Take 2–3 quick answers only. Set up Activity 01: "Now you're going to script one boundary you actually need." | Watch for: people who name a boundary that belongs to someone else's life, not theirs — redirect gently to their own.
Activity 01 · 20 min

Write your
boundary script

Activity 01 20 min

Pick one real boundary you need to set. Script it — so it's ready when you actually need it.

  1. Individual — 12 minutes silent writing
  2. Pairs — read your script aloud, once — 6 minutes
  3. Group — "What made this hard to write?"
Activity 01 (20 min) — Watch for: people who write an apology instead of a boundary · people who soften the boundary until it disappears · people who pick something low-stakes to avoid the real one. | Care note: if someone's boundary involves a difficult relationship, don't push for more detail than they offer — stay with what they've written, not what's underneath it.

Consequences

A boundary without a consequence
is a suggestion.

A consequence isn't a punishment — it's the thing you actually do when the boundary is crossed. Without it, the boundary is just a request people can decline.

  • Clear — no ambiguity about what happens
  • Kind — stated as truth, not as a threat
  • Consistent — you follow through every time, not just when you're angry
  • Realistic — something you'll actually do
08
Concept (10 min): "The consequence is what makes the boundary real." Read the four qualities slowly. Ask: "Which of these is hardest for you — clear, kind, consistent, or realistic?" Take 2–3 answers. Set up Quick Hit 02.

Quick Hit 02 (5 min): 60 seconds silent. 2 minutes pairs. Collect 3–4 from the room. Common answers: leaving the room, ending the call, not picking up a task, saying no without re-explaining. Use them to frame: "The consequence doesn't need to be big. It needs to be true." Move to Activity 02.
Activity 02 · 20 min

Choose the one
you'll actually honour

Activity 02 20 min

Pick one boundary you'll actually honour this week. It can build on your script from Activity 01, or be a different one entirely.

  1. Individual — 10 minutes: finalise the script and the consequence
  2. Pairs — say it out loud as if the moment has already come — 6 minutes
  3. Group — "What's the smallest version of holding this?"
Activity 02 (20 min) — Watch for: people choosing the safest, lowest-stakes boundary to avoid the real one · people who finalise a consequence they won't actually enforce · people who apologise while stating the script aloud. | Care note: if the boundary is about a specific person in someone's life, reflect their own words back — don't offer opinions about that relationship or that person.
