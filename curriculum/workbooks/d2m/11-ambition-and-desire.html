Desire is data

Desire isn't indulgent.
It's instructive.

Most people are more comfortable with obligation than desire. Obligation feels responsible. Desire feels risky — like wanting something means you might not get it, or worse, that you shouldn't have wanted it at all.

But desire is information. It tells you what wants to grow, what wants to change, what wants to be expressed.

The desire you're afraid to admit

The one you're afraid to admit
usually has the most energy.

It's the one that changes things — which is exactly why it's easy to bury it in vague language, cross it out, or leave it for last.

You don't have to act on it. You just have to admit it's there.

06
Concept (7 min): Read slowly: "The one you're afraid to admit usually has the most energy." Pause. | Watch for: deflection, jokes, sudden busyness with their notebook. This is often the desire people leave for last on their list of ten — don't chase it down publicly; let people arrive at it privately. Set up the desire map: "Now let's sort what you've got."
The desire map

Not all desire is
the same shape.

Go back to your list of ten. Sort each one into a category. Some will cluster — that clustering is information too.

Five categories:

  1. Creative — what wants to be made
  2. Professional — what wants to grow or shift
  3. Relational — what wants more closeness or distance
  4. Experiential — what wants to be lived, not just planned
  5. Emotional — what wants to be felt, or felt less
07
Concept (8 min): Give the five categories clearly, then let people sort their own list silently — this doesn't need group discussion. | Watch for: everything clustering in "professional" — that's common and worth naming gently: "Notice if one column is doing all the work."

Ambition without burnout

Ambition is a
system, not a sprint.

Desire tells you what you want. Ambition is what happens when you decide to pursue it. Pursued without a system, ambition burns you out and calls it commitment.

Sustainable ambition requires:

  1. Support — people who carry some of the weight
  2. Pacing — a timeline that isn't punishment
  3. Boundaries — what you protect to make room for this
  4. Rest — built in, not earned after collapse
  5. Clarity — knowing why, so effort doesn't drift
09
Concept (10 min): "Ambition is a system, not a sprint." Give all five requirements — don't skip rest or boundaries, they're the two people mentally cut first. | Watch for: people who treat this as a productivity checklist rather than a support structure. Redirect: "This isn't about doing more. It's about not burning out doing the thing that matters."

What support would make your ambition sustainable?

Write it down. Don't edit it.

60 sec silent write · 2 min pair share · 3–4 from the room

Quick Hit 02 (5 min): 60 seconds silent. 2 minutes pairs. Collect 3–4 from the room. Common answers: childcare, a co-conspirator, money, permission, someone checking in. Use them to bridge into Activity 02: "Let's build support into the map, not bolt it on after."
Activity 02 · 20 min

Map one
ambition.

Activity 02 · Ambition Map 20 min

Choose one ambition — it can come straight from your desire inventory. Map it fully before you commit to it.

  1. Individual — 12 minutes: map why it matters, what it requires, what it costs, what support you need, and your first micro-step
  2. Pairs — share the cost and the micro-step — 6 minutes
  3. Group — "What surprised you about the cost?"
Activity 02 (20 min) — Watch for: People who skip the "cost" field entirely · people whose micro-step is actually a whole plan — shrink it further · people who name support they'd need but have never asked for. | Reframe: "Naming the cost isn't talking yourself out of it. It's what makes the ambition sustainable instead of secretly resented."
Debrief 02 · 10 min

Choose one.

"What did mapping the cost make clear?"
"What support are you not currently asking for?"
"What does your first micro-step make possible?"
