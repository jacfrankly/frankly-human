# Build the Capability. Earn the Credibility. That's the Career.

Here's my actual claim: designers who build real product management capability — and get recognised for it — will build stronger, more credible leadership careers than designers who don't.

Not "designers should communicate more like product people." Not "learn to speak the business's language." Those are true, but they're the easy, cosmetic version of a much more useful idea.

The real version has two parts, and most advice for design leaders only ever addresses one of them.

**Capability** is actually being able to do product thinking — prioritising under real scarcity, making commercial tradeoffs, sequencing a roadmap when you can't do everything at once. Not performing it in a meeting. Doing it, with real constraints, until it's a rep you've built rather than a slide you've memorised.

**Credibility** is being *recognised* as capable of it by the people who decide who gets the seat at the table. You can have real capability and still not have credibility — plenty of designers quietly carry sharp commercial judgment nobody around them has ever seen tested. And you can perform credibility — the language, the confidence, the framing — without the capability underneath it, which works right up until someone asks a follow-up question that exposes the gap.

The designers who build genuinely durable leadership careers have both. They've done the reps — real prioritisation calls, real tradeoff decisions, real accountability for a roadmap that didn't work out — and they've built a track record visible enough that people trust them with bigger decisions because of it.

Why now, specifically? Because AI has compressed the execution layer of both design and product — the pixels, the specs, the tickets, all faster and cheaper than two years ago. What's left is judgment. And judgment doesn't respect the old boundary between "design's job" and "product's job." It goes to whoever can hold the whole decision, credibly, because they've actually made decisions like it before.

This isn't a call to become a product manager instead of a designer. It's a call to stop treating product capability as someone else's discipline to defer to, and start treating it as a deliberate part of your own leadership development — built through real reps, not borrowed language.

That's the thesis Design-Led is built on now: not credibility as a communication skill, but credibility earned the only way it's ever actually earned — by building the capability first.
