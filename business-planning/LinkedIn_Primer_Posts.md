# LinkedIn Primer Posts — Pre-Conference

Four posts to run in the two weeks before the Melbourne conference — two aimed at the Leader audience (Posts 1–2), two at the Practitioner audience (Posts 3–4). None of them name a program or ask for anything — the job is to plant a pattern so it's already on people's minds before you meet them in person. If any land, they also give you a natural opener: "I wrote about this last week, actually."

Space them out — roughly every 3–4 days, alternating audiences rather than running both Leader posts back to back. That keeps your feed from reading as "designer" one week and "design leader" the next, and instead threads both audiences the whole way through.

---

## Post 1 — The Silent Expert

You know the moment.

Someone in the room says something you disagreed with twenty minutes ago in your head. You had the counter-argument ready. You didn't say it.

Not because you were wrong. Because somewhere along the way, you learned that offering a strategic opinion uninvited costs more than staying quiet does.

I see this constantly in senior designers — the ones with the sharpest instincts in the room, who've quietly stopped using them unless asked.

It's not a confidence problem. It's a pattern. And it's learnable out of, the same way it was learned into.

If this is landing — where does it show up for you? Is it a specific room, a specific person, or has it become the default?

## Post 2 — Why designers still don't get the room

A designer I know built a design system with 90% adoption. Engineering loved it. Genuinely excellent work, by any measure.

Then the company set its three-year strategy — and no one thought to ask her into the room.

The instinct is to say: do better work, get more credibility, earn the seat. But I've stopped believing that's the actual mechanism.

Influence in most organisations runs through a few specific things — who controls budget, who shapes the roadmap before it's already decided, who's trusted enough to be briefed early instead of after. And in plenty of workplaces, there's a fourth one nobody likes to say out loud: who you know, who sponsors you, who owes you.

Great work is necessary. It's not sufficient. And I think most leadership advice for designers pretends otherwise.

Curious what this looks like from the inside of your organisation — is it the work that's not landing, or the room that's not opening?

## Post 3 — On time, under budget, still wrong

A product team I worked with shipped a new onboarding flow. On time. Under budget. Every box ticked.

Drop-off at step two got 34% worse.

Nobody had asked why.

Here's the thing — every design decision actually lives in three places at once: is it wanted, does it work commercially, can it actually be built. Most teams are fluent in the third one. Fewer are fluent in the first.

The onboarding flow was entirely feasible. Nobody had checked whether it was desirable.

I ask designers to score their last real project against all three, honestly, not just the one their team is strongest in. It's uncomfortable. It's also the fastest way to see where judgment quietly got skipped under deadline pressure.

Where does your team's strength actually sit — desirability, viability, or feasibility? And where does it usually get skipped?

## Post 4 — The mindset you know you're avoiding

Ask any designer to name the mindsets good design work requires and they'll rattle them off easily: empathy, systems thinking, bias to action, comfort with ambiguity, constant iteration, clear communication of intent.

Ask which one is hardest to actually *practise* where they work, and the room goes quiet for a second.

That pause is the interesting part. Not because people don't know the answer — because most people have never been asked to say it out loud.

For some it's bias to action, in an org that reviews everything to death. For others it's embracing ambiguity, in a culture that wants certainty before anyone will fund the work.

Naming it doesn't fix it. But you can't fix what you won't name.

Which one's hardest for you, honestly — and is that about you, or about the room you're in?
