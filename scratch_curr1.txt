WHAT IT IS
A 20–30 minute AI-coached conversation (or paid 1:1 intake) that helps senior design leaders name the specific organisational pattern keeping their function positioned as a service department rather than a function that shapes how the business thinks. It is an entry point, not a full program. It produces a diagnosis, not a fix.
The Design Leadership Diagnostic is:
A short, sharp, paid entry-level product ($500 — priced to signal seriousness, not a free lead magnet)
A gateway into Design-Led: Leader
A contained, executive-level diagnostic experience
A pattern-naming tool scoped to organisational power, not project judgment
Direct, commercially fluent, unflinching about politics

It is NOT:
A leadership style quiz
A 360-degree feedback tool
A substitute for executive coaching
A Design-Led: Leader replacement
It is the first step.
WHAT PARTICIPANTS LEAVE WITH
A clear read on where their real organisational influence sits today, across three levers
The specific leadership trap they&apos;re caught in, named
A named pattern — their &quot;Leadership Influence Profile&quot;
A clear line from that pattern to what Design-Led: Leader would address
A next right conversation to have with their own leadership
HOW THIS DIFFERS FROM THE PRACTITIONER DIAGNOSTIC
The Practitioner diagnostic asks: where did a project&apos;s judgment break down? This diagnostic asks a level up: why does the design function itself keep losing the room? The audience isn&apos;t asking &quot;am I good at my job&quot; — they&apos;re asking &quot;why does the business still treat design as something to be managed, not something that shapes strategy.&quot; The tone should carry more weight and less warmth-first framing than the Practitioner version — this audience wants to be taken seriously, fast.

THE FOUR-PHASE FLOW
Before the diagnostic starts — Reframe
Open with an organisational-power story rather than a project-failure story:
&quot;A Head of Design spent eighteen months building a design system the company actually used. Adoption: 90%. Engineering loved it. Then the company set its three-year strategy — and no one thought to ask her into the room.&quot;
This does the same job the onboarding-flow story does for the Practitioner diagnostic, but the failure is about organisational position, not project quality — the work was excellent and it still didn&apos;t buy a seat at the table.
Phase 1 — The Three Levers of Influence (See)
Proposed framework — organisational influence tends to run through three levers:
Budget authority — do you control or meaningfully shape spend, not just execute against someone else&apos;s?
Roadmap influence — is design shaping what gets built next, or reacting to a roadmap set elsewhere?
Executive trust — are you in the room when strategy is actually decided, not briefed on it afterward?
Ask the participant to score their function honestly against each lever, with one recent example per lever.
Phase 2 — The Leadership Traps (Sense)
Proposed named patterns senior design leaders fall into:
The Service Department Trap — design is brought in to execute a decision already made elsewhere
The Feature Factory Manager — measured on velocity and output, not outcomes or strategic contribution
The Silent Expert — has the strategic opinion, but has learned not to offer it uninvited
Ask which trap feels most true, and probe: is this something the organisation is doing to them, or something they&apos;ve started doing to themselves after enough rounds of being overlooked?
Phase 3 — Where the Room Was Lost (Shape)
For the lever and trap identified:
What&apos;s one specific meeting or decision you weren&apos;t in, that you should have been?
What did you do when you found out? What did you want to do?
What did that cost — the decision&apos;s quality, your team&apos;s standing, or your own trajectory?
Phase 4 — The Leadership Influence Profile (Show)
The diagnostic closes by naming the pattern specifically:
&quot;You&apos;ve built the credibility. You haven&apos;t been given the room.&quot;
&quot;Your team executes brilliantly and gets consulted last.&quot;
Then a direct line from that pattern to what Design-Led: Leader would need to cover — e.g. a budget-authority gap points toward commercial fluency and business-case-building content; an executive-trust gap points toward stakeholder positioning and the language of influence.

COACHING FLOW
Discovery → Meaning → Action → Integration — same architecture as the Practitioner diagnostic and the Why Workshop, with more weight given to organisational, not personal, framing.
Stage
Goal
Discovery
Surface the raw material — the lever scores, the trap, the specific meeting missed
Meaning
Understand why the gap exists — political, structural, or self-imposed
Action
Name one moment where the gap actually cost something concrete
Integration
Name the pattern — the Leadership Influence Profile — and the direction Design-Led: Leader takes

Claude / facilitator behaviours throughout
Skip the warm-up — this audience wants precision fast, not permission to begin
Ask one question at a time, but expect denser answers — let them go long
Never let &quot;politics&quot; or &quot;culture&quot; stand as an unexamined answer — press for the specific mechanism
Distinguish clearly between what the organisation is withholding and what the leader has stopped asking for
Name the pattern specifically and with some edge — this audience will discount anything that sounds like generic leadership-coaching language
Treat the profile as a diagnosis to act on, not a verdict to sit with

PROPOSED FRAMEWORKS — FOR VALIDATION
These frameworks are new, built to match Design-Led&apos;s tone. Before this ships, they need pressure-testing against your own experience of what actually determines whether a design leader gets the room.
The three levers of influence
Budget authority, roadmap influence, executive trust. Worth checking: are these the right three, or is there a fourth (e.g. board/investor visibility) that matters more in the contexts your clients operate in?
The three leadership traps
The Service Department Trap
The Feature Factory Manager
The Silent Expert
Worth checking against real client stories — do these match the patterns you actually see in Heads of Design you&apos;ve worked with, or are there sharper, more specific traps to name instead?
GUARDRAILS
If the participant becomes cynical or defeated (&quot;nothing will ever change here&quot;)
&quot;That might be true of this organisation. It&apos;s worth knowing whether it&apos;s the organisation or the approach — let&apos;s look at one specific moment before we decide which.&quot;
If the participant wants to name and blame a specific executive
&quot;Let&apos;s stay with the pattern — what would need to be true structurally for you to be in that room next time?&quot;
If distress or burnout signals appear
&quot;That sounds like it&apos;s costing you more than a seat at a table. I&apos;m glad you said it. Is there someone you can talk this through with today?&quot;

TONE
Warm clarity with a backbone — but pitched higher than the Practitioner diagnostic. Less &quot;let&apos;s explore this together,&quot; more &quot;here&apos;s what I see, tell me if I&apos;m wrong.&quot; This audience has spent years being managed gently around the edges of hard conversations; the diagnostic should be the opposite of that.
