# Style Guide — Jacinta McMahon Teaching Practice

*One document. Voice + visual. Apply to every deck, workbook, run sheet and post built from this practice unless explicitly overridden.*

*Last updated: May 2026 · Applies to: DESIGN-LED · Design to Me (D2M) · Service Blueprinting · Frankly Human*

---

## 00 · The Anchor

> **Warm clarity with a backbone.**

Grounded, human, modern, systems-literate, emotionally intelligent. Never corporate. Never cold. Never generic "design-system."

Every rule in this document — voice, type, colour, layout — serves that phrase.

## 03 · Audience & Register

### DESIGN-LED
Senior designers and design leads in large organisations (financial services, tech, consulting). Technically skilled. Underutilised. Want more influence. Don't yet speak the organisation's language.
**They need to feel:** seen, challenged, equipped. Not lectured at. Not given theory they can't use Monday.

### Design to Me
Mid-career professionals — often women, often in high-achieving environments — competent by every measure, quietly unsure if this is the life they meant to build.
**They need to feel:** understood, not fixed. Given tools, not prescriptions.

### Service Blueprinting
Mixed-discipline teams, often in financial services.
**They need:** to leave with something they can actually use — a clearer picture of their service and a list of things to fix. Not a new framework.

### Tone modifiers by format

| Format | Warmth | Edge | Playfulness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Workshop slides (DESIGN-LED) | Medium | High | Low–medium |
| Facilitator run sheets | Medium | High | Low |
| Participant workbooks | High | Medium | Low |
| D2M materials | High | Low–medium | Low |
| LinkedIn / marketing | Medium | High | Low |
| Programme descriptions | Medium | Medium | Low |

"Playfulness" means the light wink — the sentence that subverts the expectation. Not jokes. Earned surprise.

## 04 · Voice — What Each Word Means

**Warm** — human, not clinical. Acknowledges that this is hard. Knows the room has seen things, tried things, been let down by things. Doesn't perform warmth — earns it through specificity and honesty.

**Clarity** — one idea at a time. Short sentences. Strong verbs. No jargon that hasn't earned its place. Test: could someone who isn't a designer understand this on first read?

**Backbone** — doesn't hedge. Doesn't qualify everything. Takes a position. Says "this is wrong" when something is wrong. Respects the audience enough to tell them something uncomfortable.

## 05 · The Six Core Patterns

These are the sentence and structural moves that define the voice. Use consistently.

### Pattern 1 · The two-part declaration
Short declarative. Then the harder truth.
> *Research isn't a phase. It's a habit.*
> *Structure is invisible until it breaks.*
> *The best design decision in the room means nothing if you can't land it.*

The second sentence raises the stakes — not a synonym.

### Pattern 2 · The specific provocation
Open with a real story. Specific numbers. A named outcome. Not a hypothetical.
> *Meridian Bank shipped a new onboarding flow. On time. Under budget. Drop-off at step 2 was 34% worse than the old one. Nobody had asked a single customer why they were leaving.*

Use a named fictional org (see §11). Specific numbers are not optional. End with the failure — the lesson comes later.

### Pattern 3 · The parallel contrast
Two versions of the same thing. Shows the gap.
> *Feature teams own roadmaps. Empowered teams own outcomes.*
> *A report says: here is everything we measured. A dashboard says: here is what you need to decide.*

Both sides specific. Don't tip the reader off too early. The contrast must reveal something.

### Pattern 4 · The list that earns its existence
Lists only when items are genuinely parallel and independent. Max six. Usually three or four.
> *The interview has three parts:*
> *— Intro: set context, get consent*
> *— Body: open questions, follow tangents*
> *— Conclusion: summarise, invite correction*

Don't use bullets to break up paragraphs that should just be written.

### Pattern 5 · The direct instruction
Imperative. Numbered. Specific. No hedging.
> *Write 5 interview questions. Rules: no yes/no answers, no solution embedded, at least one probes emotion or context. 12 minutes.*

Tell people what to do, not what they "might consider doing." Numbered, not bulleted. State the time.

### Pattern 6 · The honest debrief question
Not *"how did that go?"* — a question with a specific edge.
> *"Which question had the solution hidden inside it?"*
> *"What did you choose to show — and what did you choose to hide? Why?"*

Always in quotation marks on slides. Always a question the facilitator genuinely wants the answer to. Never answered for the participant.

## 06 · Writing Rules

### Sentences
- **Short.** More than two clauses → cut in half.
- **Active voice.** *"Design earns its value through judgment"* — not *"value is earned through design judgment."*
- **Strong verbs.** *"The team decided"* — not *"the team made a decision."*
- **Em dashes (—) are encouraged** as a pivot, not a comma replacement.
- **Contractions are fine** — they're, it's, doesn't, you'll. Reads more human.

### Paragraphs
- One idea per paragraph.
- Body paragraphs: max four sentences.
- Slides: max two sentences per body block.

### Numbers
- Numerals for data: *34%, six months, 400 interviews.*
- Round numbers are suspicious — specific numbers earn trust.
- Always give context: *"34% worse — not slightly worse, not meaningfully worse. 34%."*

### Capitalisation
- Module titles in full caps: **DESIGN-LED · MODULE 01 · FOUNDATIONS**
- Section labels in small caps or consistent styling: **SECTION 01**
- Slide titles: title case *or* sentence case — pick one per deck.
- Not all caps for body text.
- ALL CAPS labels for categorical tags (CLARITY · ACTION · BACKLOG).
- Numbered prefixes (01, 02, 03) for sequential steps.

### Punctuation
- Em dash (—) not en dash (–) for pivots and asides.
- Oxford comma: yes, always.
- Exclamation marks: no. If it isn't exciting, punctuation won't fix it.
- Ellipses: only where a pause genuinely earns the attention.

## 07 · Visual Identity

### Personality (visual)
Warm · Clear · Human · Grounded · Modern · Calm · Confident · Slightly playful.
Never corporate · Never cold · Never generic "design-system."

### 7.1 Colour palette

**Primary**

| Token | Use | Suggested value |
|---|---|---|
| Warm charcoal | Body text, headings | `#2A2724` |
| Soft white | Primary background | `#FAF7F2` |
| Warm sand / oatmeal | Secondary background, blocks | `#EFE7D8` |

**Accents** (used sparingly, one per slide is usually enough)

| Token | Use | Suggested value |
|---|---|---|
| Deep olive | Frames, lane labels, primary accent | `#4F5B3C` |
| Soft clay | Highlights, soft fills | `#C97B5B` |
| Muted teal | Diagram emphasis, secondary accent | `#5A7A78` |
| Warm mustard | Underlines, single-word emphasis (sparingly) | `#C8A14A` |

**Tone:** grounded, earthy, human. Not neon. Not tech-blue. Not gradient-heavy.

**Rules:**
- Backgrounds default to soft white. Use warm sand for section openers and quote moments.
- One accent per slide. Two only if one is structural (e.g. olive frame) and one is emphasis (e.g. mustard underline).
- No saturated brights. No corporate blue. No black-on-white — always warm charcoal on soft white.

### 7.2 Typography

**Headings** — modern humanist sans serif, weight Medium or Semibold.
Suggested: **Söhne**, **Calibre**, or **Inter** (in that order of preference).

**Body** — clean sans serif, weight Regular.
Pair with the heading family or use Inter for body in all decks for consistency.

**Tone:** readable, warm, modern. Not corporate. Not display-y.

**Sizing (1920×1080 slides):**
- Section labels: 18–22 px, tracked +40, ALL CAPS
- Slide titles: 56–80 px, Semibold
- Body: 28–36 px, Regular
- Captions / footnotes: 18–22 px

Minimum slide text size: **24 px**. Never smaller.

**Sizing (workbooks / print):**
- H1: 32 pt · H2: 22 pt · Body: 12 pt minimum.

### 7.3 Layout principles

- **Generous white space.** If a slide feels full, cut something.
- **Clear hierarchy.** One title, one body block, one optional visual or diagram.
- **One idea per slide.** Always.
- **Title at top left.** Body content left-aligned. No centred slabs of body text.
- **Soft rounded shapes.** Border-radius on cards and frames. No harsh geometry.
- **Subtle system-like lines** to hint at structure — thin olive or charcoal rules, never heavy.
- **No drop shadows. No heavy gradients. No corporate blue. No dense text blocks.**

### 7.4 Iconography

- Simple line icons. Rounded corners. Warm, human feel.
- Stroke weight consistent across a deck (1.5–2 px at icon native size).
- Used sparingly — to label, not to decorate.
- No corporate icon packs. No filled glyph fonts.

### 7.5 Illustration

- Micro-illustrations only. Hand-drawn feel. Soft, organic lines.
- Minimal colour — usually charcoal line on warm sand, or single accent.
- Used to add warmth, not decoration. One per slide maximum.
- IDEO-style metaphor library is the reference: magnifying glass (research), compass (framing), funnel (synthesis), lightbulb (insight), arrow-through-stages (process), 3-circle Venn (desirable/feasible/viable). See §12.

### 7.6 Photography (if used)

- Real people. Real workplaces.
- Natural light. Human moments. No staged poses, no stock-photo smiles.
- Warm tonal grade — never cool blue.

### 7.7 Blueprint diagram style

- Clean lines. Soft corners. Warm neutrals.
- Clear lane labels in deep olive or warm charcoal, ALL CAPS, tracked.
- Minimal colour — use accent only to highlight a single failure point or moment of truth.
- Human-centred annotations — short, in voice.
- High clarity over visual richness.

## 08 · Format-Specific Rules

### Workshop slides
**Per slide:**
- One headline (the claim) — short declarative or two-part pattern. Never a topic-heading sentence.
  *Wrong:* "The Importance of Research in Product Development"
  *Right:* "Research doesn't slow you down. Bad decisions slow you down."
- One body block — 2–4 short sentences or a tight list. Never prose paragraphs.
- One optional visual or diagram concept.

**Slide types:**
- **Section opener** — warm sand background, large numeral (01), section title.
- **Concept** — soft white, headline + 2–3 sentence unpack.
- **Activity** — bold the rules. State the time. Imperative only.
- **Debrief** — one to three questions in quotation marks, generous space.
- **Close** — action-oriented, energising. Not summary-heavy.

### Facilitator run sheets
Written for the facilitator, not participants.
**Voice register:** slightly more personal than slides. Occasional first person. Uses **Ask:** and **Listen for:** as labels.

**Per beat:**
- Slide reference
- What to say (or read aloud)
- What to ask
- What to listen for / anticipated responses
- Facilitation note if the room goes sideways

Confident, practical. Doesn't over-explain what a good facilitator already knows.

### Participant workbooks (D2M)
**Voice register:** highest warmth. Most personal. Closest to one-on-one.
**Feel:** *a letter to someone who is figuring something out.*
**Structure:** Prompt → space → reflection → takeaway.
**Prompt style:** *"Write the brief you've been unconsciously following."* Specific, direct, not scary.
**Never:** diagnostic language, clinical terms, numbered scoring systems that pathologise.

### LinkedIn / marketing
**Voice:** professional but human. Commercially confident. Never corporate bland.
**Structure:** short post, strong opener, earned close.

**Does not do:**
- Humble-brag formatted as insight
- Thread-bait structured as false tension
- "Unpopular opinion:" openers
- Lists of ten things you need to know

**Does:**
- Short observations with a sharp point
- Real questions she actually has
- Specific things she's noticed or learned that others haven't named yet

### Programme descriptions
**Structure:**
1. The claim (one sentence — what this programme believes)
2. Who it's for (specific — not everyone)
3. What you'll leave able to do (3–4 items, observable)
4. Format note (half-day, online, cohort, self-paced)

**Avoid:** "This programme will help you...", "You'll gain valuable insights...", passive voice throughout, any sentence that could apply to any programme.

## 09 · What to Avoid

### The corporate consultant register
> ~~"This module is designed to help participants leverage design thinking to drive better business outcomes."~~

Tells: *designed to help*, *leverage*, *drive outcomes*, passive construction.
**Fix:** *"Design thinking is a decision-making tool. This module teaches you how to use it when it matters most."*

### AI-generated enthusiasm
> ~~"Great question! This is such an important topic." / "In today's fast-paced world..." / "At the end of the day..."~~

**Fix:** Nothing. Don't write filler. If there's nothing to say, don't say it.

### The hedge spiral
> ~~"It might be worth considering whether perhaps the team could explore..."~~

**Fix:** *"The team needs to explore this."*

### The passive insight
> ~~"Research has shown that teams who conduct regular user interviews tend to make better product decisions."~~

**Fix:** *"Teams that talk to users every week make better decisions than teams that run one big research sprint at kickoff."*

### The vague emotional claim
> ~~"This is a powerful experience that will transform the way you think about design."~~

**Fix:** Show it. Don't claim it. Write the provocation that earns the transformation.

### Visual slop
- Drop shadows on cards
- Heavy gradients (especially purple/blue)
- Stock corporate icons
- Three accent colours fighting on one slide
- Centred body text
- Text smaller than 24 px on a slide
- Hand-drawn SVG attempts at complex illustration — use a placeholder instead
- Emoji as decoration

### Jargon without payoff
**Allowed if immediately unpacked:** *empowered teams · continuous discovery · opportunity solution tree · service blueprint · vibe coding*
**Not allowed without serious justification:** *ecosystem · leverage (verb) · synergies · holistic approach · digital transformation journey · best-in-class*

## 10 · DESIGN-LED vs Design to Me — Register Differences

Same anchor. Different register.

| | DESIGN-LED | Design to Me |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary feeling** | Equipped, challenged, seen | Understood, given permission, held |
| **Sentence edge** | Sharp | Gentler |
| **Provocation style** | Business scenario, data, named failure | Personal pattern, unnamed recognition |
| **Activity tone** | *"Write five interview questions. Rules:"* | *"Write the brief you've been unconsciously following."* |
| **Visual accent bias** | Deep olive, muted teal, mustard for emphasis | Warm sand, soft clay, more white space |
| **What it never does** | Tells you what to feel | Diagnoses, prescribes, fixes |

D2M is softer in tone — not weaker in backbone. The backbone shows up in the questions it refuses to soften.

## 10.1 · Design Language for Non-Designer Audiences (D2M)

The D2M audience are not designers. Design language lands beautifully as *metaphor*. It alienates as *jargon*.

**The rule of thumb:**

| If it sounds like… | Then… |
|---|---|
| A tool | Simplify it. ("Affinity mapping" → "group the ideas that feel similar.") |
| A mindset | Keep it. ("Prototype your life.") |
| A process | Translate it. ("Ideate" → "generate possibilities.") |
| A designer talking to designers | Drop it. ("Divergent thinking" → no.) |
| A designer talking to humans | Keep it. ("What's the smallest experiment you could run?") |

### Keep — these land

Prototype · Experiment · Next right move · Constraints · Systems · Friction · Patterns · Leverage points · Future self · Design your life · Make life flow · See → Sense → Shape → Show → Sustain

Questions that work: *"What's the least amount of work we need to learn the next most important thing?" / "What's the smallest experiment you could run?" / "What's the constraint you need to design with, not against?"*

These are sticky. People repeat them. They become part of their vocabulary.

### Soften or translate

| Instead of | Use |
|---|---|
| Ideation | "generate possibilities" |
| Synthesis | "make sense of what you see" |
| Divergent thinking | "open up the options" |
| Convergent thinking | "choose what matters" |
| Affinity mapping | "group the ideas that feel similar" |
| Problem framing | "name the real problem" |
| Systems mapping | "map the parts of your life that interact" |

### Avoid entirely (D2M general audiences)

UX terms · product terms · service design terms · anything that sounds like a corporate workshop, design sprint, or Figma tutorial. The audience doesn't need it. The methodology doesn't need it.

**The signature:** Design thinking → translated into human language → delivered with emotional intelligence.

You're not teaching design. You're teaching agency.

## 13 · Reference Examples

### Slide headline
**Good:** *Structure is invisible until it breaks.*
**Bad:** *The Role of Information Architecture in Product Design*

### Provocation
**Good:** *Meridian Bank's executive team gets a 47-slide data pack before every weekly meeting. Decisions still get made on gut feel. Nobody is lying — the data is real. But nobody knows which number to trust.*
**Bad:** *Many organisations struggle with data overload, which can make it difficult for leaders to make informed decisions.*

### Activity instruction
**Good:** *Write a brief for an AI coding tool to build one component of Foundry's developer portal. Include: the user, the goal, the data it displays, the design constraints. Write the brief only — not the code. Focus on intent, not implementation. 12 minutes.*
**Bad:** *In this activity, you'll have an opportunity to explore how you might approach writing a brief for an AI tool. Feel free to be creative with your response.*

### Debrief question
**Good:** *"Which question had the solution hidden inside it?"*
**Bad:** *"How did the activity go for everyone? Did anyone want to share what they came up with?"*

### Programme description opener
**Good:** *DESIGN-LED is leadership training for people who happen to be designers.*
**Bad:** *DESIGN-LED is a comprehensive programme designed to equip design professionals with the skills and mindset needed to succeed in today's complex organisational landscape.*

### Slide composition
**Good:** Soft white background. Title top-left, 64 px Semibold warm charcoal. Two-sentence body block, 32 px Regular, left-aligned. A thin warm mustard underline beneath one word in the title. A single magnifying-glass micro-illustration in the lower right.
**Bad:** Centred title in display serif over a purple-to-blue gradient. Three icons in a row. Drop shadow on a card containing six bullet points. Blue accent. Helvetica.

## 14 · Working with AI Tools

When briefing AI (Claude, Copilot, others) to draft or refine content:

**Brief on:**
- Format (slide, run sheet, workbook, marketing)
- Module and scenario organisation
- Section type (provocation, concept, activity, debrief, close)
- Voice anchor: *warm clarity with a backbone*

**Ask for:**
- Two-part declaration headlines
- Specific numbers and named organisations
- One idea per slide
- Imperative activity instructions with numbered rules

**Check against:**
- §09 (What to Avoid)
- §13 (Reference Examples)
- The question: *does this sound like Jacinta, or like a corporate training module?*

**Red-flag phrases to find and remove:**
*designed to help participants · leverage · drive outcomes / drive alignment / drive change · in today's fast-paced world · holistic approach · best-in-class · gain valuable insights · empower (as verb applied to participants by the programme)*
