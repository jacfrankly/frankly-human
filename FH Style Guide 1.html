02 · Two Registers, One Practice

Editorial. Earthy.

Same anchor. Different rooms. The editorial register goes to the boardroom and the workshop. The earthy register goes to the kitchen table. Pick one per artefact. Don't mix mid-document.

Register A · Editorial

Show up.
Make it land.

For: DESIGN-LED · Service Blueprinting · LinkedIn · Programme decks

Soft white paper, sharp ink, monospace labels, single bright accent. Magazine-grade type. Big confident numbers. One idea per slide. No drop shadow, no gradient, no filler.

Register B · Earthy

Mind yo'self.
Take your time.

For: Design to Me · Workbooks · Reflection prompts · Newsletters

Soft sand paper, warm charcoal, more white space, hand-drawn moments, lower-case section titles. Olive and clay accents. Slower rhythm. The same backbone — just gentler in the room.

07 · Content & Voice

How it sounds.

Warm clarity with a backbone. Short sentences. Strong verbs. Specific numbers. Named scenarios. No filler. Take a position.

Six patterns that recur

Pattern 01
Two-part declaration
"Research isn't a phase. It's a habit."
Short declarative. Then the harder truth. The second sentence raises the stakes — not a synonym.
Pattern 02
Specific provocation
"Meridian Bank shipped on time. Drop-off at step 2 was 34% worse. Nobody had asked a customer why."
Named org. Specific numbers. End with the failure — the lesson comes later.
Pattern 03
Parallel contrast
"Feature teams own roadmaps. Empowered teams own outcomes."
Two versions of the same thing. Both sides specific. The contrast must reveal something.
Pattern 04
List that earns it
"Three parts: intro, body, conclusion. Each independently meaningful."
Only when items are genuinely parallel. Max six. Usually three or four is right.
Pattern 05
Direct instruction
"Write 5 questions. Rules: no yes/no, no solution embedded, at least one probes emotion. 12 minutes."
Imperative. Numbered. Specific. State the time. No hedging.
Pattern 06
Honest debrief
"Which question had the solution hidden inside it?"
Always in quotes on slides. A question the facilitator genuinely wants answered. Never answered for the participant.

Good · Bad

Good · headline

Structure is invisible until it breaks.

Two-part declaration. Specific verb. Raises the stakes. Reads in one breath.
Bad · headline

The Role of Information Architecture in Product Design

Topic heading, not a claim. Capitalisation imposed. No verb. Could be the title of any deck.
Good · provocation

Meridian Bank's exec team gets a 47-slide pack every Monday. Decisions still get made on gut feel. Nobody knows which number to trust.

Named org. Specific number. Names the failure. Stops at the failure — the lesson comes later.
Bad · provocation

Many organisations struggle with data overload, which can make it difficult for leaders to make informed decisions.

Vague subject. Hedge ("can make it difficult"). Reads like a textbook chapter opener. Solves nothing.
Good · activity

Write 5 interview questions. Rules: no yes/no, no solution embedded, at least one probes emotion. 12 minutes.

Imperative. Numbered constraint. Time stated. Trusts the participant to do the work.
Bad · activity

In this activity, you'll have an opportunity to explore how you might approach writing some questions.

Hedge spiral. No constraint. No time. The instruction softens itself out of existence.
Good · debrief

"What did you choose to show — and what did you choose to hide? Why?"

Specific edge. The facilitator genuinely wants the answer. Cannot be answered with "it went well."
Bad · debrief

"How did the activity go for everyone? Did anyone want to share?"

Open to the point of saying nothing. Lets participants off the hook. No direction, no tension.
Good · programme opener

DESIGN-LED is leadership training for people who happen to be designers.

One sentence. Specific claim. Not a description of what the programme does — a statement of what it believes.
Bad · programme opener

DESIGN-LED is a comprehensive programme designed to equip design professionals with the skills and mindset needed to succeed in today's complex organisational landscape.

Could apply to any programme. Passive. Hedge words everywhere. The sentence earns no trust.
07b · Writing Rules

The mechanics.

Sentence-level rules that apply everywhere. Not style preferences — rules.

Sentences
Short. Active. Specific.
  • Short. More than two clauses → cut in half.
  • Active voice. "Design earns its value through judgment" — not "value is earned through design judgment."
  • Strong verbs. "The team decided" — not "the team made a decision."
  • Em dashes (—) encouraged as a pivot, not a comma replacement.
  • Contractions are fine — they're, it's, doesn't, you'll. Reads more human.
Paragraphs
One idea. One paragraph.
  • One idea per paragraph. No exceptions.
  • Body paragraphs: max four sentences.
  • Slides: max two sentences per body block.
Numbers
Specific. Never rounded.
  • Numerals for data: 34%, six months, 400 interviews.
  • Round numbers are suspicious — specific numbers earn trust.
  • Always give context: "34% worse — not slightly worse, not meaningfully worse. 34%."
Capitalisation
Consistent. Earned.
  • Module titles: full caps — DESIGN-LED · MODULE 01 · FOUNDATIONS
  • Section labels: small caps or consistent styling — SECTION 01
  • Slide titles: title case or sentence case — pick one per deck.
  • ALL CAPS for categorical tags only (CLARITY · ACTION · BACKLOG).
  • Not all caps for body text.
Punctuation
Em dash yes. Exclamation no.
  • Em dash (—) not en dash (–) for pivots and asides.
  • Oxford comma: yes, always.
  • Exclamation marks: no. If it isn't exciting, punctuation won't fix it.
  • Ellipses: only where a pause genuinely earns the attention.
AI Briefing
Red-flag phrases to remove
  • designed to help participants
  • leverage · drive outcomes · drive alignment
  • in today's fast-paced world
  • holistic approach · best-in-class
  • gain valuable insights
  • empower (as verb applied to participants)
07c · Format-Specific Rules

Right room, right voice.

Same anchor — warm clarity with a backbone — applied differently depending on whether you're in the boardroom, the workshop, or the kitchen table.

Workshop slides
One claim. One unpack. One visual.

Per slide: one headline (the claim), one body block (the unpack), one optional visual. Never prose paragraphs.

Headline rule: short declarative or two-part pattern. Never a topic-heading sentence.

  • Section opener — warm sand background, large numeral, section title
  • Concept — soft white, headline + 2–3 sentence unpack
  • Activity — bold the rules. State the time. Imperative only.
  • Debrief — 1–3 questions in quotation marks, generous space
  • Close — action-oriented, energising. Not summary-heavy.
Facilitator run sheets
For the facilitator. Not the room.

Slightly more personal than slides. Occasional first person. Uses Ask: and Listen for: as structural labels.

Per beat:

  • Slide reference
  • What to say (or read aloud)
  • What to ask
  • What to listen for / anticipated responses
  • Facilitation note if the room goes sideways
D2M workbooks
A letter to someone figuring something out.

Highest warmth. Most personal. Closest to one-on-one.

Structure: Prompt → space → reflection → takeaway.

Prompt style: "Write the brief you've been unconsciously following." Specific, direct, not scary.

Never: diagnostic language, clinical terms, numbered scoring systems that pathologise.

LinkedIn · marketing
Professional. Human. Not corporate bland.

Short post, strong opener, earned close.

  • ✗ Humble-brag formatted as insight
  • ✗ Thread-bait as false tension
  • ✗ "Unpopular opinion:" openers
  • ✗ Lists of ten things you need to know
  • ✓ Short observations with a sharp point
  • ✓ Real questions she actually has
  • ✓ Specific things noticed that others haven't named
Programme descriptions
Four parts. One claim. Specific people. Observable outcomes.

Structure: (1) The claim — one sentence, what this programme believes. (2) Who it's for — specific, not everyone. (3) What you'll leave able to do — 3–4 items, observable. (4) Format note.

Avoid: "This programme will help you…" · "You'll gain valuable insights…" · passive voice throughout · any sentence that could apply to any programme.

07d · Register Differences

Same anchor. Different rooms.

DESIGN-LED goes to the boardroom and the workshop. Design to Me goes to the kitchen table. Pick one register per artefact. Don't mix mid-document.

DESIGN-LED · Editorial Design to Me · Earthy
Primary feeling Equipped, challenged, seen Understood, given permission, held
Sentence edge Sharp Gentler
Provocation style Business scenario, data, named failure Personal pattern, unnamed recognition
Activity tone "Write five interview questions. Rules:" "Write the brief you've been unconsciously following."
Visual accent bias Ember · Cobalt · Lime for emphasis Warm sand · Soft clay · More white space
What it never does Tells you what to feel Diagnoses, prescribes, fixes

D2M is softer in tone — not weaker in backbone. The backbone shows up in the questions it refuses to soften.

Scenario organisations (DESIGN-LED)

All DESIGN-LED content uses de-identified fictional organisations. Always name the org. Always a concrete, systemic failure — never a vague challenge, never about an individual.

08 · What we don't do

No filler. No tells.

If a thing makes the work look or sound like it came from a corporate template, an AI default, or a stock template — cut it. The list:

×
Wordsleverage · drive outcomes · holistic approach · best-in-class · gain valuable insights · designed to help · in today's fast-paced world · empower (verb)

PunctuationExclamation marks. If it isn't exciting, punctuation won't fix it.

TypeDisplay serifs on body. Italic display headings. Helvetica. Hand-drawn body copy. Anything below 24 px on a slide.
×
ColourCorporate blue. Purple-to-blue gradients. Three accents fighting on one slide. Pure black on pure white.
×
EffectsDrop shadows. Heavy gradients. Glow. Bevel. Reflection. Anything from 2008.
×
LayoutCentred body copy. Six bullets fighting for attention. A title that's a complete sentence about the topic.
×
ImageryStock photos with smiles. Hand-drawn SVG attempts at complex illustration. Emoji as decoration.
×
Content patternsHumble-brag posts. "Unpopular opinion:" openers. Lists of ten things. Filler like "great question." The corporate consultant register. The hedge spiral. The vague emotional claim.
