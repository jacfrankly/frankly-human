## 0. What I was solving for

The strategy doc gives me three specific, load-bearing constraints, not vibes:

1. **Dinesh (Persona 2.2) will not tolerate a 45-minute self-discovery workshop.** He wants precision, fast, or he bails.
2. **The tone brief:** "should feel like being read accurately by someone who's done the job, not like a personality quiz." This is a register, not an aesthetic — I read it as *warmth that comes from specificity, not from softness*.
3. **The open question I was asked to help resolve:** how a named pattern (Section 0.2 and 5 of the strategy doc — "The Feature Factory" vs. "Strong on Craft, Invisible at the Table") can be presented so it reads as *earned* — like a diagnosis — rather than gimmicky, like a quiz result, while still being memorable enough to repeat.

I went looking for places outside software where a specific, high-stakes, one-line verdict is delivered to one person, about one case, by someone who did the work first. That search kept landing in three rooms: the doctor's office, the tailor's fitting room, and the sports coach's sideline. All three share a structure worth stealing: *dense, specific observation first, then one plain sentence that names the thing — delivered without hedging and without spectacle.*

## 1. Mood board — 8–12 references, curated

**1. The pathology report "DIAGNOSIS:" field.**
What it is: the standard architecture of a clinical pathology or radiology report — a page of dense, technical, entirely unglamorous observation (specimen description, measurements, staining detail), terminating in one short, bolded, unhedged line: "DIAGNOSIS: Invasive ductal carcinoma, grade 2." Why it's here: this is the single most direct real-world analogue to what Phase 4 needs to do. The report doesn't build suspense or entertain — it *earns* the bolded line by showing its work first, then states the conclusion plainly, once. What it contributes: the exact hierarchy discipline this touchpoint needs — evidence in quiet type, verdict in loud type, and nothing decorative touching either.

**2. Savile Row tailor's chalk marks during a fitting.**
What it is: the white tailor's chalk laid directly onto dark wool during a fitting — a single line at the shoulder seam, a small cross at the waist dart. Why it's here: it is warmth and precision in the same gesture. The tailor doesn't explain the mark; the client feels it land because it is drawn on *their* body, once, correctly. What it contributes: the idea that precision itself can be the warm gesture — the opposite of Frankly Human's instinct to soften a hard truth with extra copy. One mark, well placed, is the kindest thing in the room.

**3. Sotheby's / Christie's auction catalogue condition report.**
What it is: the terse paragraph accompanying a full-page reproduction of a painting, stating flaws in plain language — "craquelure to upper right quadrant; retouching visible to background under UV." Why it's here: it is the best real-world model I found for Ade's persona (2.3), the one case where the honest read partly implicates the subject. The catalogue never softens a flaw to protect the work's value — and paradoxically that honesty is what makes the whole document feel trustworthy. What it contributes: proof that specificity of the flaw (not vagueness, not over-reassurance) is what makes bad news land as respect rather than judgment.

**4. WSET-style blind wine tasting deduction.**
What it is: the structured method a sommelier uses in blind tasting — nose, palate, structure, assessed one at a time in a fixed sequence — arriving at a single named conclusion (grape, region, vintage) stated with confidence, not hedged as a guess. Why it's here: this is a structural, not just typographic, reference — it maps almost exactly onto the diagnostic's own architecture (Three Lenses → Six Mindsets → Cause Attribution → named pattern). What it contributes: permission for the Phase 4 reveal to sound *certain*, because the method visibly earned it — the opposite of a horoscope's hedge-everything language.

**5. EKG paper strip.**
What it is: the actual physical printout from an electrocardiogram — a continuous waveform on graph-ruled thermal paper, with a stamped interpretation at the edge. Why it's here: it is a document you are handed, that is unmistakably *about you specifically* (your own waveform, no one else's), rendered in the coldest possible medium (thermal printer, grid paper) yet felt as intensely personal. What it contributes: a texture and motion cue — a "printout" register that could inform how the profile card differentiates itself from the chat log around it, without borrowing generic dashboard chrome.

**6. Friday Night Lights — Coach Eric Taylor's halftime and post-game talks.**
What it is: the recurring scene type in this TV series where the coach delivers a specific read of one player's actual performance in the game just played — never generic ("play harder"), always anchored to one real moment ("second quarter, you took your eyes off the safety before the snap"). Why it's here: it's the clearest filmic version of "warm and blunt at once, about a specific real event" that the strategy doc's Design Principle 1 (one project, one thread) demands. What it contributes: tone reference for copy, and a reminder that the warmth is *in* the specificity, not layered on top of it as a softer register.

**7. Peterson Field Guide "diagnostic marks" callouts.**
What it is: the small, precise typographic captions in field guides (Peterson, Sibley) pointing an arrow at the exact feather bar or eye-ring that distinguishes one species from a near-identical other — literally captioned "diagnostic" in the ornithological literature. Why it's here: the word "diagnostic" already means, in this tradition, *the one specific visible feature that removes ambiguity* — which is precisely the job of this tool's named pattern. What it contributes: a model for how to visually mark "the specific detail that makes this reading certain" rather than presenting the verdict as an unsupported assertion.

**8. Aesop packaging and product copy.**
What it is: the apothecary-adjacent retail brand's combination of a clinical ingredient list (INCI names, percentages) with one dry, literate sentence of description, on kraft-toned paper stock, no photography of people. Why it's here: it is the strongest existing commercial proof that "warm and precise" can coexist without tipping into either coldness or preciousness — and it does it entirely through restraint and paper/ink texture, categories close to Frankly Human's own paper/ink palette. What it contributes: permission to let restraint itself carry warmth, rather than adding hand-drawn softness to compensate.

**9. Bloomberg Businessweek feature headline treatment (Richard Turley era, c. 2010–2014).**
What it is: the magazine's habit of running a single, oversized, declarative headline sentence as cover or spread type — often in a bold serif or condensed grotesque at a size that dominates the page, with all supporting reporting set small beneath it. Why it's here: it is an editorial precedent for "one sentence gets all the typographic weight in the room" that isn't a UI pattern, and isn't twee — it's blunt, confident magazine design. What it contributes: a model for exactly how much size and space disparity between the "tag" and everything else on the close screen can look like confidence rather than a marketing banner.

**10. Boxing corner-work between rounds (documentary/broadcast framing, e.g. HBO's 24/7 series).**
What it is: the sixty-second real-time footage of a trainer, close to a fighter's face, giving two or three flat, specific instructions ("he's dropping his right after the jab, make him pay for it") — no pep talk, no metaphor, the clock visibly running out. Why it's here: it's the sharpest available reference for delivering a hard, specific read under real time pressure without ceremony — directly useful for how Phase 3's four sequential questions and the guardrail moments should *feel* paced. What it contributes: pacing and restraint cue — the corner-man never repeats himself and never softens the instruction, he just says it once, closer than is comfortable.

**11. Le Corbusier's Modulor system.**
What it is: Le Corbusier's proportioning system built from the measurements of the human body, used to derive architectural dimensions — a rigorous, published, numerical system whose entire premise is that precision measured *from a person* produces buildings that feel humane rather than cold. Why it's here: it's the cleanest argument I found against the false choice between "warm" and "precise" — the strategy doc's Design Principle 5 tension. What it contributes: a conceptual permission slip — the diagnostic's structure (lenses, mindsets, cause attribution) can stay rigorous and numbered internally while still producing something that reads as humane, exactly because the rigor is in service of one specific person's body of work.

**12. NFL broadcast "Next Gen Stats" card.**
What it is: the on-screen graphic overlay used in NFL broadcasts pairing one hard number (e.g., "9.7 yards of separation") with one short analyst sentence of interpretation beneath it, appearing for a few seconds during live play. Why it's here: it's a live, contemporary, mainstream example of the tag-plus-sentence architecture the strategy doc is already hypothesising toward in Section 0.2 — proof the pattern reads as authoritative rather than gimmicky when the number/tag is genuinely earned by the data feed behind it. What it contributes: direct precedent for the two-tier reveal — but also a caution (see Section 4) about how easily this exact pattern curdles into scoreboard-chrome if the "data" half looks like a UI stat tile instead of an editorial observation.

## 2. Reference collection — 15–20, organised by theme

### Typography
- Pathology report "DIAGNOSIS:" line — dense body copy terminating in one bolded, unhedged sentence (see Mood Board #1).
- Radiology report "IMPRESSION:" field — same architecture, slightly more narrative ("Impression: no acute process; findings most consistent with...") — useful for phrasing the diagnostic sentence itself, not just its typographic treatment.
- Wine Spectator / Cellar Tracker tasting note plus score — a short lyrical sentence ("a wine of extraordinary tension") sitting directly beside a hard number, an alternate tag/sentence pairing to the sports-broadcast one.
- Peterson/Sibley field guide diagnostic-mark captions — small caps, arrow-anchored, pointing at one specific visual feature (Mood Board #7).
- Bloomberg Businessweek (Turley-era) oversized declarative headline sentence set against small supporting body text (Mood Board #9).
- The New Yorker's drop-cap and pull-quote treatment in "Talk of the Town" — a single sentence pulled out and set larger mid-column, still restrained (serif, no colour), useful for how a pull sentence can gain weight without leaving the editorial register.
- Auction catalogue condition report paragraph style — plain, unadorned, declarative sentences describing flaws without hedging (Mood Board #3).

### Texture
- Savile Row chalk marks on dark wool (Mood Board #2).
- EKG thermal-paper printout, grid-ruled with a stamped interpretation (Mood Board #5).
- Aesop's kraft-toned packaging and ingredient-list typesetting (Mood Board #8).
- A tailor's paper fitting tag pinned to a garment with a safety pin, hand-written in grease pencil, trade abbreviations only — physical, unglamorous, entirely functional.
- A sommelier's or cupping-taster's chalkboard notes at a tasting bar — chalk on slate, terse abbreviations, immediate and provisional-looking rather than "finished."

### Spatial composition
- Pathology/radiology report page hierarchy — scarcity of emphasis as the hierarchy device: almost everything on the page is the same quiet weight, and that quietness is what makes the one bolded line register as loud.
- Auction catalogue spread — a very large reproduction of the artwork paired with a small, dense paragraph in the margin; scale disparity between "the thing itself" and "the note about it" is the device, directly relevant to Design Principle 3 (the profile card must dominate the close screen).
- NFL Next Gen Stats overlay — number + one line, appearing briefly over live footage, then gone; a useful negative case for motion (see Section 4 — do not let the reveal feel like a stat card that clears itself).
- WSET tasting-note worksheet layout — sequential boxes (appearance / nose / palate / conclusion) building toward one final named-wine line at the bottom, a literal template for "structured evidence, then named conclusion" that maps onto the diagnostic's own phase sequence.
- Boxing corner framing — extreme physical closeness between two people, one giving a short flat read to the other, the crowd/clock still running behind them; useful as a mood reference for the guardrail moments specifically (Phase 3), where the interaction should feel unencumbered by chrome per Design Principle 6.

### Colour
- Clinical report restraint — black ink on white/cream stock, with at most one stamp or signature in a single accent colour (often red or blue); argues for treating the plum/pink accents in the Why Workbook palette as scarce, reveal-only moments rather than throughout the diagnostic phase.
- Tailor's chalk — a single mark colour (white or blue chalk) against a dark, textured ground; argues for the profile card using one accent colour maximum against paper, not the full four-colour system at once.
- A wine's own colour in the glass, held to a white background at a tasting, as the only "swatch" needed to represent an entire structured verdict — a minimalist colour-as-evidence idea, relevant if the design-lead explores any colour-coding for the three causes (skill/organisational/relationship) in Phase 2b.

### Motion
- Friday Night Lights' halftime-talk pacing — a beat of quiet before the coach speaks, then the read delivered once, without repetition (Mood Board #6).
- Boxing corner-work real-time pressure — instructions given once, closer than comfortable, no do-overs (Mood Board #10).
- The physical act of a tailor drawing a chalk line — a single continuous, unhurried stroke, not a series of taps or fills; a strong candidate for how the profile tag itself could visually "arrive" on screen (see Section 4's direct recommendation — this connects to an asset the codebase already has).
- An EKG needle's steady, monotone trace suddenly producing one identifiable spike — useful tempo reference for pacing Phase 3's four sequential questions: mostly steady, then one moment (the actual named pattern) stands out because everything before it was even.

## 3. Aesthetic direction brief

This touchpoint should feel like the ten seconds after a doctor turns the monitor toward you and says one plain sentence instead of a paragraph of hedges — not because the news is soft, but because they've clearly already done the work of finding the right sentence, and they respect you enough not to pad it.

It should feel like a tailor's chalk mark: made once, in silence, exactly where it needs to be, on a body they've actually looked at — not sketched twice and erased, not accompanied by reassurance about how you're "still finding your style." The warmth is in the accuracy, not in the tone of voice around it.

It should feel like the sixty seconds in a fighter's corner between rounds: someone leaning in close, telling you the one true thing about what just happened in the round you actually fought — not a pep talk about fighting harder, not a metaphor, one flat specific sentence about the jab you keep dropping your hand after. The clock is running. There is no room for throat-clearing.

Concretely, against the Why Workbook's existing system: keep the paper, the ink, the DM Serif Display gravity, the Fraunces editorial warmth — this is still Frankly Human, still coaching-register, not a re-skin into the colder frankly@work/lime register. But turn the dial toward restraint everywhere the Why Workbook allowed itself decoration: fewer hand-drawn blob shapes, no Allura signature flourishes during the diagnostic itself (Allura is a closing-credit gesture, not a working-session one — reserve it, if used at all, for a single moment on the Close screen, not the diagnostic phases), and let quiet, consistent typographic weight do the work that illustration did in the Why Workbook. The four-colour system (teal/pink/yellow/plum) should behave like a clinician's single accent stamp, not a palette in constant use — one colour, used once, at the moment it's earned, reads as more confident than four colours used decoratively throughout.

The single sensory image to hold: a chalk line on dark wool. Precise, hand-made, warm because it was made by someone who looked at you first, and entirely without ornament.

## 4. Direct recommendation — typography for the Design Influence Profile reveal

The strategy doc's open question (Section 0.2 and 5): tag-only risks reading as a personality-quiz archetype ("the Architect"); sentence-only is hard to say out loud or repost. My research supports the two-tier hypothesis the strategist already floated, but with a specific typographic and structural treatment, borrowed directly from the pathology-report and auction-condition-report references above, plus two things to actively avoid.

**Do this:**

1. **Borrow the report architecture, not the badge architecture.** Set the tag in `DM Serif Display` italic, in `--plum`, at a size closer to the site's `.ww-title` treatment than to any chip/pill/card component already in the codebase. Do not give it a background fill, a border-radius, or a container shape — no card, no pill, no icon. A plain horizontal rule above and below (1px, `--ink`) is enough framing, exactly like a report's field divider. This matters specifically because the app's own visual language already uses filled, bordered, rounded-corner shapes (`.value-chip`, `.vm-state-btn`) to mean "selectable game piece, one of several options." Reusing that shape for the final verdict would visually tell the user "this is one of several possible results you could have gotten" — which is the exact quiz-result reading the tone brief rejects. The verdict should look like the one thing on the page that is not a UI control.

2. **Name it like a chart entry, not a quiz result.** Recommend the tag be phrased as a diagnostic noun phrase in the register of Persona 2.1–2.3's own descriptors already in the strategy doc — "The Underpowered Craftsperson," "The Isolated Operator" — rather than a punchy compound coinage in the 16Personalities/BuzzFeed register ("The Feature Factory" leans this way and should be treated as the riskier end of the naming spectrum, not the default). A diagnosis name should sound like something a specialist would write on a chart, not something optimised for a Slack emoji reaction. Content-writer's call on final wording, but the visual treatment should not do anything (colour-coded badge systems, iconography, "collect them all" framing) that pulls the name toward archetype territory.

3. **Cite the evidence directly beneath the tag, in the participant's own words.** The auction condition report never asserts a flaw without locating it ("craquelure to upper right quadrant"); the diagnostic should do the same. Beneath the tag, set the full diagnostic sentence in `Fraunces` italic (matching `.ww-lede`'s treatment) — but include, as part of that sentence or immediately after it in a smaller `Inter` line, one specific quoted fragment from the participant's own Phase 1a project description or Phase 3 answer. This is the single highest-leverage move for making the tag feel earned instead of generic: a personality quiz result is identical for anyone who picks the same options; a diagnosis that visibly cites *your* project, in *your* words, cannot be mistaken for one.

4. **Stage the reveal in two beats, using an asset the codebase already has.** Per the WSET/pathology-report structural pattern (evidence before conclusion) and the boxing-corner/tailor pacing references (say it once, don't repeat it): have the evidence sentence fade in first (reusing the existing `msgIn` 0.2s animation already defined in `why-workshop-app-v2/index.html`), pause briefly, then have the tag arrive with a single underline stroke drawn beneath it in `--plum`, using the `.mark-underline` SVG primitive already built in `style-guides/Visual Style Guide.html`. That primitive is currently used for inline emphasis on a phrase within body copy; repurposing it here as the one animated gesture on the entire close screen — a single hand-drawn stroke landing once, like a chalk mark — ties the mood board's tailor reference directly to an asset that already exists, rather than introducing new decoration. Use it exactly once, on the tag alone, nowhere else on the diagnostic.

**Avoid:**

- Any resemblance to the NFL Next Gen Stats overlay's *temporariness* — that reference is useful for the tag/sentence pairing shape but wrong for permanence; the profile card is the artifact the participant keeps, not a graphic that clears itself after a few seconds.
- Colour-coding the tag by which of the three causes (skill/organisational/relationship) produced it. It's tempting because Phase 2b already captures that as structured state (Design Principle 2), but a colour-coded result category is a half-step back toward "type," and the strategy doc is explicit that this must never read as a category system. Keep the cause-attribution color logic, if any, internal to the Phase 2b component; let the Phase 4 reveal be colour-restrained per Section 3 above.

## 5. Handoff note to the design-lead

The three references I'd want you to keep pinned above your monitor while you build the close screen: the **pathology report** (#1), the **auction condition report** (#3), and the **chalk mark** (#2). Between them they answer the strategy doc's open question directly — report architecture for how evidence-then-verdict should be typeset and sequenced, the condition report for why citing the participant's own specific words is what makes a diagnosis feel earned rather than generic, and the chalk mark for the one thing I'd want you to carry through every decision on this touchpoint: **precision itself is the warm gesture here — don't add softness to compensate for directness, the directness is the kindness.**

Two things in this research created real tension worth surfacing rather than resolving myself, because they're design calls, not scouting calls:

- The Why Workbook's existing visual system leans decorative (painted blob shapes, Allura signature flourishes, four colours in regular rotation) and this touchpoint's brief explicitly asks for something sharper and more contained *within* that same system. I've recommended dialling those elements back significantly during the diagnostic phase and reserving them (if at all) for the Close screen — but "how much Why Workbook DNA survives before this stops reading as the same app" is exactly the judgment call the strategy doc already flagged in its Open Questions (Section 5, second bullet) about the `vm-state-btn` reuse, and it applies here too, one level up, at the whole-touchpoint level.
- Reference #12 (NFL Next Gen Stats) and reference #9 (Bloomberg headline treatment) point in slightly different directions on how much size disparity is confident versus how much starts to look like marketing. I lean toward Bloomberg's editorial register over the broadcast-graphic register — worth a second opinion before committing, since it's a genuine judgment call rather than something either reference resolves outright.

Nothing in the Why Workbook's shipped taste anchor surprised or contradicted me — the DM Serif Display / Fraunces / restrained-ink-and-paper foundation already has everything this sharper register needs in it. The job was subtraction (fewer blobs, fewer colours in simultaneous use, no quiz-shaped containers) more than addition.
