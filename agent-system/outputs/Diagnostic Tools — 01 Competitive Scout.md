# Diagnostic Tools — Competitive Scout Report

Prepared by: Design Scout (DSc)
For: Design Strategist, ahead of Design Thinking Diagnostic + Design Leadership Diagnostic
Extends: `business-planning/Diagnostic_Tool_Build_Notes.md` (Plan B Engine teardown) — not repeated here, only referenced where it changes the read on a new product.
Also referenced directly: `why-workshop-app-v2/index.html` + `app.js` — the Why Workbook, the architectural model both new diagnostics are meant to follow (4-phase chat, `PHASE_LABELS`-driven progression, embedded special components like `values-sort`/`values-map` chip UIs, a closing "card" result + `d2m-bridge` continue-elsewhere handoff + email capture). I'm treating this as the internal baseline every external product gets compared against, not as one of the 4–6 audited products itself.

Note on method: several of the products below are JS-heavy SPA quiz tools that resist automated fetching mid-flow (their marketing pages describe the product; the live question-by-question interaction isn't scrapable). Where I couldn't observe the live interaction directly, I've said so and relied on documented UX patterns for that product instead of guessing at specifics I didn't verify.

## 1. Competitive audit

### 1.1 16Personalities (MBTI-style, free)
**What it is:** The single most-taken personality test on the internet. ~60 questions, Likert-scale agree/disagree, produces one of 16 named types (e.g. "the Campaigner," "the Architect") plus a Role and Strategy grouping.

**What it does well:**
- The **name is the product.** Nobody says "I got INFP" in casual conversation — they say "I'm a Campaigner." Naming the result as a persona rather than a code is the single highest-leverage move in the whole category, and it's exactly the move Frankly Human is already committed to (Why Statement, and now "Design Influence Profile" / "Leadership Influence Profile"). Worth studying *why* their names land: they're one memorable noun with an article ("the Architect"), not a compound adjective-noun ("Analytical Innovator"). That's a naming-convention decision the design-strategist should make deliberately for both new profiles.
- Free tier is generous enough to feel complete (multi-paragraph type description, strengths/weaknesses, relationship and career sections) before any paywall appears — the paywall sits *behind* a satisfying free result, not in front of it. This is the opposite of Plan B Engine's own good instinct (surface the handoff early) applied to monetisation instead of navigation: give away enough that the free result is shareable and complete on its own.
- Result is built for social proof loops — it's optimized to be posted, argued about, and re-taken by friends. That's not this tool's job (Frankly Human diagnostics aren't going viral), but the underlying mechanic — a result specific enough that the person wants to show someone else — is worth keeping as a design goal for the "Profile" outputs.

**What it does poorly / entrenched-pattern risk:**
- Zero adaptivity — it's a fixed-length static questionnaire wearing a conversational skin. There's no "chat," just single questions rendered one per screen. Because it's so ubiquitous, users will unconsciously benchmark ANY named-result tool against its pacing (roughly 1 question per 10–15 seconds, ~60 in under 12 minutes) — if either Frankly Human diagnostic feels slower than that per-exchange, it will read as sluggish even though the chat format is doing genuinely more work per turn.
- The type is a life-long fixed label with no state/context capture ("how is this showing up right now") — contrast with the Why Workbook's `values-map` component, which explicitly captures aligned/activated/absent *right now* per value. That distinction (trait vs. current state) is one of the most defensible reasons a Frankly Human diagnostic should NOT just be "16Personalities for designers" — the whole value proposition of a coached diagnostic over a static quiz is capturing where someone actually is, not just what type they permanently are.

### 1.2 Gallup CliftonStrengths
**What it is:** 34-theme, paid, ipsative (paired-statement, forced-choice) assessment, industry-standard in corporate L&D. ~30 minutes, immediate Top 5 reveal, full 34-theme report available at a higher price tier.

**What it does well:**
- **Strengths-only framing, deliberately.** No "growth areas," no weaknesses section in the core report — it only names what's already working and tells you how to lean into it further. This is a real strategic choice, not an omission, and it's the opposite of a gap-analysis / maturity-score tool. Directly relevant to the Leadership Diagnostic: a $500 product for senior leaders should probably borrow this instinct over a deficit-framed "here's what's wrong with your leadership" framing — senior audiences are typically more resistant to being told what they lack and more receptive to being told what's real and under-leveraged.
- Paired-statement forced choice ("I read instructions carefully" vs "I like to jump right into things," pick one and rate intensity) is a genuinely different mechanic from both Likert scales and chip-sorts — it captures relative priority without asking "which 3 matter most" directly, which is worth considering as a third interaction pattern alongside the Why Workbook's tap-to-circle-then-star and Plan B Engine's tap-to-rank.
- Tiered depth (Top 5 free-with-purchase vs full 34 at higher price) is a legitimate monetization pattern for a "gateway to a paid program" tool — the free/first-tier diagnostic answer doesn't need to be the *complete* answer, it needs to be complete enough to be useful and specific enough to create appetite for the next tier.

**What it does poorly:**
- The report is dense corporate-HR-speak once you're past the initial theme name — long enough that most people read their Top 5 headline and never finish the full PDF. A specific, named, short-form output (closer to the Why Workbook's single-screen `why-card`) will out-perform Gallup's own report on completion/read-through, which is a real advantage Frankly Human should lean into rather than trying to match Gallup's depth.
- No conversational or coaching layer at all — it's the purest "wizard, not chat" product in this set, and it still succeeds as the industry standard. This is useful ammunition against the assumption that chat-based necessarily beats wizard-based: Gallup proves a static, well-designed questionnaire with a strong naming/output layer can carry enormous credibility with zero adaptivity. The adaptivity of a chat format needs to visibly earn its cost in build complexity, not be assumed superior by default.

### 1.3 Enneagram tests (Truity / Crystal Knows / generic) — as a category
**What it is:** ~100-180 statement tests producing a numbered type (1-9) plus a "wing," with a defining "core motivation" or "avoidance pattern" and documented growth/stress directions.

**What it does well:**
- The **motivation-first framing** ("what are you moving away from," not just "what are you like") is more psychologically active than trait-based tests like MBTI. For a "Design Influence Profile," this is a genuinely useful structural idea: naming not just a designer's style but what they're unconsciously avoiding (e.g., avoiding conflict with engineering, avoiding being seen as "just visual") would be sharper and more diagnostic than a static style label.
- Growth/stress arrows (how you behave under pressure vs. in security) is a dynamic-state layer bolted onto a static type — closer to what a coached conversation can do naturally that a one-shot quiz has to simulate. This is a pattern Frankly Human's chat format can do *better* than a static quiz, because it can actually ask "and when you're under pressure, what happens to that?" as a follow-up rather than pre-scripting a lookup table for every type.

**What it does poorly:**
- Type numbers (1 through 9) without the name attached are meaningless to newcomers — "I'm a 5" communicates nothing outside the subculture that already knows Enneagram. The named descriptor ("the Investigator") is doing all the communicative work, the number is vestigial. Reinforces: whatever naming scheme gets chosen for the two new profiles, the name itself needs to carry full meaning standalone, because it's what gets remembered, said out loud, and put in a LinkedIn bio — the underlying framework/methodology is invisible to the end user.

### 1.4 HubSpot Website/Marketing Grader
**What it is:** Not a personality tool at all — a B2B lead-magnet "grader": enter a URL + email, get an instant 1-100 score across a few dimensions, with a PDF report and an implicit upsell into HubSpot's paid product suite.

**What it does well:**
- It is the clearest existing model in this research for the **exact commercial shape** both Frankly Human diagnostics need to perform: free diagnostic → specific, credible-feeling output → natural next-step CTA into a paid product. HubSpot's grader has driven enormous top-of-funnel volume for over a decade specifically because the score feels earned (it's actually computed from real signals about your site) rather than arbitrary.
- Numeric score (1-100) is instantly legible and comparable — you know immediately if 62 is bad. This is a completely different output shape from a named-type result, and worth flagging as a real alternative the design-strategist should consciously rule in or out: does the Design Leadership Diagnostic want a *score* (comparative, benchmarkable, slightly threatening to a senior ego) or a *name* (identity-affirming, less comparable, softer)? These produce very different emotional registers and the choice should be deliberate, not default.

**What it does poorly:**
- Email-gate-before-any-value (you must submit URL + email before seeing even a partial score) is a harder gate than anything in this research set, including Plan B Engine. It works for HubSpot because of brand trust and volume, but it's a weak pattern to imitate for a cold-traffic diagnostic without HubSpot's brand recognition — reinforces the existing note in the build docs that Frankly Human's own tools should surface value (and any save/continue mechanism) early, not gate it behind a form.

### 1.5 LinkedIn Skill Assessments
**What it is:** Timed 15-20 question multiple-choice knowledge tests (not self-report) per skill. Score in the top 30% and you get a badge for your profile; fail and nothing is shown to anyone.

**What it does well:**
- **Asymmetric visibility on failure** — LinkedIn never surfaces a failed attempt to your network or to recruiters, only passing badges display. This is a genuinely humane design decision worth naming explicitly for a senior-audience product: a $500 Leadership Diagnostic absolutely must never produce a result that reads as "you failed" or ranks the person against peers in a way that could be seen by anyone else. Self-report + no comparative failure state is the safer register for both tools, but especially the executive-facing one, where reputational stakes are much higher than a mid-weight practitioner topping up their skills.

**What it does poorly:**
- It's a pure knowledge test with a binary pass/fail outcome, no narrative, no personalization, no coaching layer. Zero of it is directly reusable for a "profile"-style diagnostic — it's included here specifically as the anti-pattern boundary: this is what a diagnostic looks like when it optimizes for credential-signaling instead of self-understanding, and Frankly Human's tools should stay clearly on the self-understanding side of that line.

### 1.6 UX-PM "Design Maturity Score"
**What it is:** The closest genuinely design-specific comparable found. An 8-10 minute self-assessment aimed at "a design professional or a business leader," framed as a "quick check-up," delivering an emailed maturity report, feeding into UX-PM's own paid certification tiers and a "Design Leaders Masterclass."

**What it does well:**
- It is architecturally almost identical in *business* shape to what's being built here: free diagnostic → emailed report → named paid program tiers, aimed partly at the same Head-of-Design audience as the Leadership Diagnostic. Worth a close look specifically for how UX-PM sequences its own upsell copy from the report into the certification funnel, since that's a live, working example of the exact funnel shape Frankly Human needs for the Design Leadership Diagnostic → $5k-$50k program.
- "Quick check-up" health-metaphor framing softens what could otherwise feel like being graded — worth considering for tone, especially on the Design Thinking Diagnostic where the audience (mid-weight practitioners) is likely to arrive with more self-doubt than a Head of Design would.

**What it does poorly:**
- Result is a "customized report" delivered by email after the fact, not revealed in-session — this reintroduces exactly the friction the Why Workbook and Plan B Engine's "continue in Claude" pattern both deliberately avoid (never show the payoff in the same session where the effort happened). This is the single most important thing to actively avoid copying from this tool: an in-session, immediate, named reveal is a real and current best practice in this exact category (see 16Personalities, Enneagram, Human Design below), and UX-PM is behind the curve on it despite being the most directly comparable product found.
- No named archetype — it's a maturity *level*, not a persona. Confirms the market gap noted below: nobody in the design-leadership space has done the naming-and-personifying move that MBTI/Enneagram/CliftonStrengths have done in the general personality space.

### 1.7 BetterUp AI Coach / Torch Leadership Labs (as a pair — enterprise AI-coaching benchmark)
**What they are:** Enterprise coaching platforms sold to companies for their employees/executives, not direct-to-consumer diagnostics. BetterUp pairs an AI chat coach with an optional "Whole Person Assessment" that's nudged, not mandatory, during onboarding. Torch pairs human coaches (many doctoral-level psychologists) with an AI agent called "Spark."

**What it does well:**
- BetterUp's choice to make its assessment **optional and nudged rather than a mandatory gate** ("Members can begin the assessment and return to complete it at a later time") is worth naming because it's the opposite of both Frankly Human diagnostics' design (which are themselves the entire product, not an optional add-on to a subscription). It's not directly applicable, but it's useful evidence that even a company with an AI coach and enterprise budget doesn't force the full assessment up front — supports keeping the diagnostic itself short (20-30 min, as scoped) rather than being tempted to extend it.
- Both platforms explicitly position a **human or human-like coach identity** ("Spark," a named AI agent) rather than a generic chatbot — worth flagging for the design-strategist: does either Frankly Human diagnostic want its chat coach to have any name/persona distinct from "Frankly Human" the brand, the way Spark is distinct from Torch? The Why Workbook currently doesn't name its coach voice; worth a deliberate decision either way for the two new tools, especially the higher-stakes, higher-price Leadership Diagnostic where an anonymous "the system" voice may read as impersonal at a $500 price point.

**What it does poorly (for this research's purposes):**
- Neither is really auditable as a stand-alone diagnostic UX — they're enterprise SaaS platforms with the assessment buried inside a broader coaching subscription, gated behind employer-paid seats. I could not observe an actual first-time-user diagnostic flow for either, only documented product-page descriptions. Flagging this limitation explicitly: if the design-strategist wants deeper detail on BetterUp/Torch's actual in-session UX, that would require either a live account or a dedicated UX-teardown pass with screen recording, which was outside what I could do here.

## 2. Pattern library

**Entrenched — expensive to break, honour these:**
- **One question/prompt visible at a time**, not a scrollable list. True of every single product audited, static or chat. Users have been trained on this by MBTI-style tests, Typeform, and now chat UIs converging on the same shape for different reasons. The Why Workbook already does this correctly via its `chat-log` + turn-based flow. Do not deviate.
- **Named result over scored result, when the audience is a person, not a business.** Every consumer-facing personality/pattern tool in this set (16Personalities, Enneagram, Human Design) uses a persona name; only the two B2B/lead-gen tools (HubSpot Grader, UX-PM Maturity Score) use a bare score or level. Since both Frankly Human tools already commit to "Profile" language ("Design Influence Profile," "Leadership Influence Profile"), this is honouring, not breaking, the pattern — but it means avoiding the temptation to also bolt on a numeric score, which would muddy which register the result lives in.
- **Immediate, in-session reveal.** Every well-regarded example (16Personalities, Enneagram, Human Design, Plan B Engine) shows the result in the same session as the effort. UX-PM's emailed-after-the-fact report is the one weak example, and it's the one thing about that tool worth explicitly not copying.
- **Free tier complete enough to be shareable/satisfying on its own**, paywall/upsell strictly behind it, not blocking it. 16Personalities, CliftonStrengths (Top 5 free), Human Design (fully free, no gate at all) all do this. Only HubSpot gates before any value at all, and that's defensible only because of HubSpot's pre-existing brand trust — Frankly Human doesn't have that yet for these two new tools, so the safer default is closer to Human Design's zero-gate model, at least for the first tier of result.

**Common but not entrenched — safe to break deliberately:**
- **Trait-only framing (who you permanently are) vs. state framing (how this is showing up right now).** Most quiz-style tools (16Personalities, CliftonStrengths, LinkedIn Assessments) only do the former. The Why Workbook's `values-map` component (aligned/activated/absent, right now) already breaks this, and it's a genuine differentiator worth carrying into both new diagnostics rather than reverting to a pure-trait model just because that's what the wider market does.
- **Wizard/fixed-sequence vs. adaptive chat.** Roughly split in this research: CliftonStrengths, 16Personalities, Enneagram, HubSpot, LinkedIn are all fixed-sequence; only the AI-coach products (BetterUp, Torch, Apt) and Plan B Engine's "Operationalise it" handoff step toward adaptivity. Fixed-sequence tools dominate by volume and are the market default, but that's a function of build cost, not proven superiority — this is genuinely undecided in the market, which is exactly the framing already noted as an open architecture question in the build notes. Nothing in this research resolves it definitively either way; if anything, Gallup's success with zero adaptivity is a caution against assuming chat automatically wins.
- **Deficit/gap framing vs. strengths-only framing.** UX-PM's "maturity score" and most leadership-quiz SEO content lean deficit ("here's your gap"). CliftonStrengths deliberately doesn't. Worth breaking toward the CliftonStrengths model, especially for the $500 Leadership Diagnostic — senior, paying, executive audiences respond worse to being told what's wrong with them from a tool they haven't built trust with yet.

## 3. Benchmark analysis — 2-3 best-in-class, worth studying in depth

**1. 16Personalities — for the *naming convention* specifically, not the mechanic.** Not because the quiz itself is well-designed (it's a static Likert questionnaire dressed as ~modern~), but because it has run the single largest live experiment in "does a persona-name register beat a code/score" that exists, and the name has won decisively for a decade-plus at massive scale. The design-strategist should study its naming grammar directly: single memorable noun + definite article ("the Architect," "the Campaigner"), not a compound descriptor. Whatever "Design Influence Profile" and "Leadership Influence Profile" ultimately name their output patterns as, they should be pressure-tested against this exact bar: would someone say it out loud, unprompted, the way people say "I'm a Campaigner"?

**2. The Why Workbook's own `d2m-bridge` + email-capture close screen (internal, but worth naming as the benchmark to beat, not just a baseline).** It already implements the single best idea found anywhere in this research (the "continue in Claude" handoff, sourced from Plan B Engine and already built) alongside a named result card and low-friction, skippable email capture ("That's okay — I'll screenshot it"). Nothing external in this research improves on that close-screen structure; the two new diagnostics should treat it as the floor, not something to reinvent. The open question is whether the *body* of the diagnostic (the chat + special components) needs new interaction patterns the Why Workbook doesn't have yet — see the CliftonStrengths paired-statement mechanic and the Enneagram growth/stress-arrow idea above as two candidates worth prototyping.

**3. Human Design quiz (JobCannon) — for how it handles credibility honestly at zero cost to trust.** Small, obscure product, but it does something none of the bigger names do: it explicitly tells the user its own methodology is "a playful proxy, not a real BodyGraph calculation" and frames the result as "a reflective prompt... not a verdict" — while still being specific, structured, and fully free with no email gate at all. This is directly relevant to Frankly Human's own stated guardrail language (never overclaim, "draft not destination"). It proves you can be maximally honest about a tool's own limits and still produce a result that feels complete and worth keeping (named type + strategy + "not-self theme" + growth edge + shareable card, no login). Worth a closer look specifically for how it structures a short, honest, non-diagnostic-overreach result — a real template for how the Design Thinking Diagnostic (the free-er, lower-stakes of the two) could frame its own honesty about being a starting point, not a clinical instrument.

## 4. Handoff note to the design-strategist

Three findings I'd actually build around:

**1. The naming convention is the highest-leverage unsolved decision in this whole project, and almost nobody in the market has cracked it for a design-specific audience.** 16Personalities/Enneagram/CliftonStrengths have all solved "trait → memorable persona name" for general audiences; nobody has done it well for designers or design leaders specifically (UX-PM's "maturity score" is a level, not a name — this is a real gap, see below). Whatever naming system gets built for "Design Influence Profile" / "Leadership Influence Profile" should be pressure-tested the way 16Personalities' names are: one memorable noun, sayable out loud, distinct enough per-result that people recognize each other's types. This matters more for the mid-weight Design Thinking Diagnostic, where the name IS the shareable/social artifact that drives word-of-mouth into the paid course; it matters less (but still some) for the Leadership Diagnostic, where the $500 price point buys more patience for a longer, richer, less bumper-sticker-able result.

**2. Immediate in-session reveal is now a hard market expectation, not a nice-to-have — and the one direct comparable that violates it (UX-PM) is worse for it.** Every well-regarded example in this research reveals its result the moment the effort is done. If either diagnostic's architecture ever drifts toward "we'll email you the Profile," that's a regression relative to both the market and the Why Workbook's own existing close screen. Worth flagging early because "email the PDF" is a tempting simplification once build pressure hits.

**3. Genuine market gap: nobody has built a strengths-framed (not deficit-framed), named-persona, design-leadership-specific diagnostic with an immediate reveal and a real paid-program handoff.** UX-PM comes closest structurally (design-specific, free→paid funnel) but is deficit-framed ("maturity"), emailed not revealed, and un-named (a level, not a persona). CliftonStrengths has the strengths-only framing and the named-result instinct but is generic, corporate, and has zero design specificity. Nobody currently combines: design/leadership specificity + persona naming + strengths framing + immediate reveal + honest-about-its-own-limits copy + a credible paid handoff. That combination is open, and it's exactly the lane the Leadership Diagnostic is positioned to take at its $500 price point against a senior audience that Gallup/16Personalities don't speak to directly and UX-PM speaks to but disappoints on delivery.

One pattern to flag as a real, deliberate choice rather than a default: **score vs. name as the core output currency.** HubSpot's Grader (numeric, comparative) versus 16Personalities/Enneagram/Human Design (named, non-comparative) are genuinely different products with different emotional registers — a score invites benchmarking against peers (useful for urgency, riskier for a senior ego at $500), a name invites self-recognition (softer, more durable, more shareable). Given both tools are already scoped as "Profile" outputs, I'd read that as an implicit decision already made in favour of naming over scoring — worth the design-strategist confirming that's intentional rather than assumed.
