# Design Leadership Diagnostic — Heuristic Evaluation

Prepared by: Heuristic Evaluator (HE)
Reviewed: `design-leadership-diagnostic-app/index.html`, `app.js`, `netlify/functions/chat.js` (static source review — no live/browser testing performed)
Against: Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics, the design principles and personas in `Design Leadership Diagnostic — 02 Strategy.md`

---

## 0. Top-line answer to the four questions the brief asked directly

**Does the tool ever let a thread score or trap selection through without a real example attached first?**
Yes, in two distinct ways — see F2 and F3 below. Trap selection (Phase 2) is recorded the instant a card is tapped, with no evidence field on the component itself; evidence only arrives afterward, in the probe conversation, and even then Claude is explicitly instructed not to block progress on a vague answer. Thread scoring (Phase 1) does require *a* non-empty evidence string before submission, but nothing checks it's a *specific, real example* rather than a placeholder word — and the system prompt tells Claude plainly: "you may note that plainly... but do not block progress." Principle 3 is enforced as a UI formality in Phase 1 and not enforced at all in Phase 2.

**What happens on a failed API call — does it match this audience's low tolerance for anything feeling cheap?**
The chat error state itself is well-built: plain-language copy, a genuine retry that resends the exact same payload, and an honest human-escalation line after two failures. That part earns its price point. But there's an inconsistency the audience would notice if unlucky: the *other* async call in the product — the PDF/email capture on Close — fails completely silently (F5), and a narrow resume-flow edge case around a failed Phase‑1 submission call can re-show an already-answered thread as blank (F6). The good and bad error-handling coexist in the same product.

**Is there any moment that reads as a "quiz" despite the strategy's rejection of that register?**
No gamification (no points, badges, levels, "unlock" language) — confirmed absent in both the HTML/CSS and the system prompt's explicit "DO NOT" list. The one soft risk is that the thread-scorer's two-button forced choice and the trap selector's three tap-cards both carry a "pick one" visual grammar associated with lightweight internet quizzes (F12) — a deliberate, strategy-sanctioned tradeoff, and a minor one, but worth a design-critic gut check.

**Does phase orientation (plain text only, no progress bar) work, or will Persona 1 feel lost?**
Mostly works. `Phase 1 of 4 · The Four Threads` is always present in the header, updates live, and in-log dividers announce phase changes without re-announcing sub-phases — a genuinely well-judged implementation of Principle 5. Where it's thinner: nothing tells her *how far into* a phase she is (e.g., "thread 2 of 4" isn't stated anywhere, she has to count the summary rows accumulating above the current card) — see the Persona 1 walkthrough, Phase 1 section.

**Does resume-session recovery work for a $500 product?**
Mostly, with one real gap. The happy-path resume (close tab between phases, reopen, click "Continue where I left off") replays the full transcript and correctly restores every phase. The gap is narrower and more specific: the exact moment between submitting Phase‑1 evidence and the next thread's opening question arriving is not crash-safe — see F6.

## 1. Cognitive walkthrough — Persona 1, full flow

*"The credible but sidelined Head of Design." 8–15 years in, has read leadership content before, can smell genericness in one paragraph, low patience for onboarding, arrived because something specific just happened.*

**Welcome.** The headline — "Why does the room keep closing without you in it?" — lands. It's a question she's actually asked herself, not a value proposition. The reassurance line ("$500 · 20–30 minutes · Result on screen the moment you finish") does the precision-not-softening work the tone brief calls for. She types her name, hits Enter (works — the Enter-to-submit binding is there), and moves on. No friction here; this screen does its one job well.

**Intake.** This is the screen the brief asked me to pressure-test hardest, and it's genuinely good writing under real content discipline — the "Is not" list (not a quiz, not 360 feedback, not coaching, not the program itself) pre-empts her exact skepticism before she has to voice it, and the privacy block is direct and unhedged. But it's a long screen for someone with "limited patience for onboarding": roughly 200+ words across four sections (is-not list, privacy block, leave-with list, secondary note), no sticky CTA, `Begin` sitting at the bottom of a scroll on any normal viewport (F10). She'll likely skim rather than read in full — the bolded lead-ins on each "is not" item and the numbered "leave with" list are scannable enough to survive a skim — but this is the one place in the flow where "impatient and skeptical" is working against "thorough enough to actually build trust," and the design hasn't fully resolved that tension. She clicks Begin.

**Reframe.** The eighteen-months/90%-adoption/no-one-thought-to-ask-her story opens cold, no greeting, ends on "Let's find yours." This is the single sharpest moment in the product for this persona — it's a mechanism, not a mood, exactly per the tone brief. If the model executes this turn as scripted (see F7 on model risk), it's a strong opening.

**Phase 1 — The Four Threads.** The intro line ("Most leadership advice only ever talks about the first three — this one doesn't skip the fourth") signals the tool knows something. The forced-choice-plus-evidence card is a good format: quick to answer, but it requires the actual example before it lets her past (`Next thread →` shakes the evidence field if empty). Completed threads collapse into a visible summary row, so she's never asked to recall an earlier answer — good recognition-over-recall design. Two things will register with a senior reader across these four repeats: (1) the interaction is structurally identical four times in a row (same two-button shape, same evidence field, same button label pattern) — the strategy doc itself flagged this exact risk ("will detect a scripted quiz faster than a junior audience would"), and the only thing carrying adaptivity between repeats is a one-sentence Claude acknowledgment that's capped at 40 words and explicitly forbidden from asking a question — thin cover if that acknowledgment ever reads generic; (2) nothing in the header states her position *within* Phase 1 — "Phase 1 of 4" doesn't become "thread 3 of 4," she has to infer progress by counting summary rows. Neither is severe alone; together they're the moment most likely to make her feel "processed" instead of "read," which is exactly the failure mode the strategy doc names as fatal for this persona.

**Phase 2 — The Three Traps.** "Three patterns account for most of what you just described. Which one is yours?" — good, assertive framing. But the mechanism here is the clearest violation of the evidence-before-judgment principle in the whole product: she taps a card, and the trap is recorded immediately (F2) — no evidence field on the component, unlike Phase 1. The probe question that asks *why* only comes after the pick is already stored. For a self-aware persona who "will detect" a scripted move, tapping the trap that "feels most true" off a one-line description, before being asked to justify it, is the moment most likely to feel like the leadership-quiz genre this audience has learned to distrust — even though the copy is serious and the card styling isn't cartoonish.

**Phase 3 — Where the room was lost.** Three fixed questions (what moment, what did you do, what did it cost), pressed once on a vague cost answer. This is the strongest-designed phase from a content-architecture standpoint — it's genuinely Socratic on paper, and the "press for the actual moment" instruction is exactly the guardrail language the strategy doc said was already well-crafted. Whether it *executes* that way live depends entirely on the model actually holding to one question per turn and genuinely distinguishing "answered" from "still vague" — see F7.

**Phase 4 — The reveal.** "Here's what I see. Tell me if I'm wrong." is a strong, confident opening line for the payoff moment. If the structured JSON parses, the reveal is built from her actual evidence and lands well. If it doesn't parse — and the prompt asks Haiku to hit five keys with two exact-substring constraints under a hard word-economy regime, a genuinely fragile ask — she silently receives a fixed, generic sentence with no indication anything went wrong (F1). Given this profile sentence is the entire deliverable of a $500 product explicitly sold as "not a personality type," a silent fallback to boilerplate in exactly this moment is the single highest-severity risk in the product.

**Close.** The reveal card, the Claude continue-bridge (genuinely pre-filled with her real evidence, not a template — this part delivers on its promise), then the email-capture block, then the Design-Led: Leader bridge. She has just spent 20–30 minutes on something she paid $500 for and now wants to save it — but the save mechanism is the third major scroll-stop on the screen, not immediately reachable, directly against the strategy doc's own requirement for this exact screen (F4). If she does try to email herself the PDF and the request fails, she gets no explanation at all — the button just resets (F5), which will read as broken precisely because the chat error-handling two screens earlier trained her to expect better.

## 2. Cognitive walkthrough — Persona 3, Intake screen only

*"The corporate buyer scoping this for someone else." An HR/L&D lead, CDO, or a design leader's own manager, evaluating from outside the target persona's lived experience — not planning to complete the diagnostic herself. Her presence is, per the strategy doc, the entire reason the Intake screen's privacy line exists, and her trust in it is named as "make-or-break for the corporate-licensing business model."**

**Will she know what to do next?** Not really — and this starts before Intake. There is no path into this app that addresses her at all. The Welcome screen's headline and both lede lines speak in second person to the participant ("You'll leave with the specific pattern keeping your function positioned..."). To even *see* the Intake screen — the one screen built partly for her — she has to type a name into "First name" and click "Start the diagnostic," i.e., role-play as the participant she's evaluating this for. There's no "for organisations," "how this works for teams," or "talk to us about a licence" link anywhere in the flow to shortcut this (F8). This is a real gap given how much weight the strategy doc puts on her.

**Will she be able to see how to do it, and will she understand the feedback?** Once she's on Intake (having posed as a participant to get there), the payoff is real: the privacy block is the one place in the entire product speaking directly to her risk. "What you say here stays with you. If this was purchased through a corporate licence, the person who bought your seat sees that a seat was used — never your answers, never your result. No exceptions." is unhedged, specific, and answers her core question in one read. This is good copy doing real work.

Where it thins out for a diligence-minded evaluator specifically: it's a single sentence with no supporting depth. No link to a privacy policy, no mention of where responses are stored, no mention of who *internally* at Frankly Human can see individual responses, no mention of data retention (F9). A buyer scoping this for a team or a corporate licence is very likely to have exactly these follow-up questions, and there is no help/documentation escape hatch anywhere in the app to answer them — she'd have to email in and ask, with nothing in the UI even surfacing that as an option on this screen.

**Will she trust it enough to buy?** The one sentence she needed exists and is well-written, which matters — but she has no way to verify it's backed by anything. The `index.html` source itself contains a code comment marking payment/session-confirmation integration as explicitly out of scope for this build ("this screen should only render its full content when a confirmed-payment state exists... Out of scope for this build"). That's a reasonable and clearly-flagged build-sequencing decision, not a design flaw — but it does mean that, as currently shipped, the "seat was used, never the answers" promise is asserted in copy ahead of any visible access-control mechanism behind it. Not a usability finding in the Nielsen sense, but worth surfacing here explicitly since the brief asked me to verify this exact linkage: right now the promise and the enforcement are not yet the same thing, and Persona 3 has no way to tell the difference from the UI alone.

**If she makes an "error" (decides not to buy)?** There's no penalty or dead end — she can simply leave. No finding here; noting it for completeness of the walkthrough only.

## 3. Findings by Nielsen heuristic

### H1 — Visibility of system status
- **F6 (Major, 3).** A narrow resume-flow gap: if the network call following Phase‑1 evidence submission fails (the call that would normally advance to the next thread), and the participant closes/reloads instead of clicking "Try again," the saved evidence for that thread is retained in `STATE.diagnostic.threads` but `restorePhase()` doesn't check for it — it re-renders the same thread's card blank via `showThreadCard(idx)` regardless. `STATE.session.phase` was already advanced to the current thread's own key *before* the evidence-submission call, not after, so on resume the app has no way to tell "thread answered, transition pending" from "thread not yet started." **Users affected:** anyone who loses connection or closes the tab in the few seconds between submitting Phase‑1 evidence and the next question arriving — plausible on mobile. **Remediation:** in `restorePhase()`'s `1-*` branch, check `STATE.diagnostic.threads[key]` for the *current* index too, not just indices `< idx`; if present, skip straight to `transitionToThread(idx + 1)` (or re-fire the pending transition call) instead of re-showing a blank card.
- No other status-visibility issues found; the typing indicator, phase label, and system dividers are consistently and correctly used elsewhere.

### H2 — Match between system and the real world
- **F7 (Major, 3).** The entire promised register — "skip the warm-up," assertions not questions, one question per turn, near-verbatim guardrail lines, an exact five-key JSON reveal with two exact-substring constraints — is carried entirely by prompt instructions to `claude-haiku-4-5`, the fastest/smallest tier, with zero code-level verification that any turn actually obeyed the format rules. This is the highest-stakes, most senior-facing, most format-sensitive touchpoint in the product line, and it's the one place using the cheapest model. The codebase already implicitly acknowledges the risk (the Phase‑4 fallback-reveal safety net exists specifically because the JSON is expected to sometimes fail to parse — see F1). Prose-quality failures (a stacked second question, a filler opener the system prompt explicitly forbids, a broken guardrail trigger) have no equivalent safety net at all. **Users affected:** all — this is a systemic risk, not an edge case. **Remediation:** at minimum, live-test Phase 1–3 turns against the "3-4 sentences, one question, no filler" constraints under real Haiku responses before launch; consider a stronger model for this specific product given its price point and audience, or add lightweight server-side validation (e.g., reject/retry a response containing two `?` characters).
- **F8 (Major, 3).** No part of the product addresses Persona 3 as a distinct user type — see the Persona 3 walkthrough above. **Remediation:** at minimum, a one-line "Evaluating this for your team or organisation?" link on Welcome or Intake pointing to a contact channel, so she isn't required to role-play as a participant to reach the one screen built partly for her.

### H3 — User control and freedom
- No findings. The "Need support?" panel is reachable at any point in the diagnostic and closes via both a button and Escape; "Start over instead" on the resume banner is a clear, low-friction exit from a stale session; changing a trap selection mid-probe is explicitly supported (silently updates the stored trap without discarding the conversation).

### H4 — Consistency and standards
- CTA copy (`Next thread →`, `Continue to Phase 2 →`, etc.) is consistent throughout. Speaker-turn treatment (cobalt rule for Claude, ink rule for participant) is applied uniformly. No findings beyond F7 above, which is really a consistency-of-execution risk rather than a consistency-of-design one.

### H5 — Error prevention
- **F2 (Major, 3).** Trap selection (Phase 2) is written to `STATE.diagnostic.trap` and sent to the API the instant a card is tapped (`handleTrapSelect`) — the component carries no evidence field of its own, unlike the Phase‑1 thread scorer. The evidence-gathering probe ("Is the organisation doing this to you, or did you start doing it to yourself...") only happens *after* the judgment is already recorded, inverting Design Principle 3's stated order ("evidence before judgment, every phase"). Even then, the probe's own instruction only presses once on a vague answer ("politics") before the phase is allowed to close. **Users affected:** all Phase‑2 participants; most acutely the self-aware, pattern-tired persona most likely to pick fast off gut resonance with a one-line card description. **Remediation:** either add a required "the example that proves it" field to the trap card itself (matching the thread-scorer pattern), or don't finalize/store the trap selection until the probe conversation has produced a real, specific answer.
- **F3 (Major, 3).** Phase‑1 evidence is required to be *non-empty* but not required to be *specific*. The server-side developer message explicitly tells Claude: "If the evidence they gave is genuinely thin or generic... you may note that plainly in the same sentence, but do not block progress — the next thread's card is coming regardless." Design Principle 3 is real in the UI (a field must be filled) and explicitly waived in the coaching logic (a one-word non-answer is accepted as final). **Users affected:** any participant who under time pressure or reticence gives a placeholder answer to move faster. **Remediation:** either let the acknowledgment turn re-open the evidence field on genuinely thin answers (soft re-ask, not a hard block, to preserve pace) or accept this as a deliberate pace-over-rigor tradeoff and note it explicitly as a product decision rather than an implicit one.
- **F11 (Minor, 1–2).** `CRISIS_KEYWORDS` client-side pre-detection only runs inside `sendMessage()` (free-text chat turns) — it is not applied to the Phase‑1 evidence textarea (`handleThreadSubmit`) or the first trap-probe submission, both of which route through `addParticipantMessage` + `callAPI` directly. Detection in those paths relies entirely on Claude's own in-the-moment judgment plus the server-side `detectDistressInResponse` phrase-match on Claude's *reply* — a real but slower and less certain safety net than the immediate client-side pre-flag. **Remediation:** run the same keyword check against thread-evidence and trap-probe text before submission, exactly as `sendMessage()` already does.

### H6 — Recognition rather than recall
- **F4 (Major, 3).** Save/export (the email-capture block) is the third major section on the Close screen — after the reveal card and the Claude continue-bridge — requiring a full scroll past two large blocks to reach. The strategy doc is explicit that this "must be reachable without scrolling past it... given a $500 purchaser has more at stake in not losing their result than a free-tool user does." As shipped, it is not reachable without scrolling past prior content. **Users affected:** all — this is the mechanism for not losing the one deliverable of a $500 purchase. **Remediation:** move email-capture above the Claude bridge (immediately after the reveal card), or duplicate a lightweight "save this" affordance near the top of the Close screen.
- Positive note: within Phase 1, completed thread answers persist as visible summary rows rather than requiring recall — good design, no finding.

### H7 — Flexibility and efficiency of use
- No accelerators exist for a returning or fast user (e.g., no way to skip the Reframe pre-phase on resume, though this is minor since Reframe has no participant input to redo). No strong finding.

### H8 — Aesthetic and minimalist design
- **F10 (Moderate, 2).** The Intake screen carries a substantial reading load (an "is not" list, a privacy block, and a 5-item "leave with" list — roughly 200+ words) with the primary CTA at the bottom of a non-sticky scroll, for a persona explicitly characterized as impatient. Individually each section earns its place (the "is not" list pre-empts real skepticism, the "leave with" list sets real expectations); together, the screen may run longer than this audience's patience budget before commitment. **Remediation:** consider condensing to the highest-leverage 2–3 "is not" items and a 3-item "leave with" list, or add a persistent/sticky CTA so committing doesn't require reading to the bottom.

### H9 — Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors
- **F1 (Critical, 4).** `enterPhase4()`'s success callback does `STATE.diagnostic.reveal = revealData || buildFallbackReveal();` — if Claude's Phase‑4 response doesn't parse as the expected fenced JSON (a real risk given the format's fragility, self-acknowledged in the prompt's own "double-check... or the reveal will fail to render correctly" comment), the participant silently receives a fixed, non-personalized `profile_sentence` ("You've built the credibility. You haven't been given the room.") and `lime_phrase` ("the room") — the exact canonical example the system prompt itself uses as a style reference and explicitly warns Claude not to reuse verbatim "unless it genuinely fits." Only `why_line` and `headline_thread` retain any real personalization in the fallback (they're derived from her actual thread/trap picks); the headline sentence itself — the single artifact this $500 product exists to produce — can be entirely generic with **zero visible indication** that this happened. This directly contradicts the product's central promise ("not a personality type... addressed to what you actually said") in exactly the failure case a skeptical, pattern-tired audience is least likely to forgive, and there is no error state, no retry offer, no disclosure — it renders with full visual confidence as if fully bespoke. **Users affected:** any participant whose Phase‑4 API call returns malformed JSON — frequency unknown without live testing, but the code's own defensive fallback proves the authors expect this to happen sometimes. **Remediation:** at minimum, detect the fallback path and surface a soft, honest note ("this read didn't fully personalize — want to try that again?") with a regenerate option, rather than presenting boilerplate as bespoke.
- **F5 (Major, 3).** `handleSubscribe()`'s failure path (non-OK response or thrown exception) does nothing but re-enable the button and reset its label back to "Send me the PDF" — no error text is shown anywhere. This sits in the same file as the well-built chat-API error state (plain copy, working retry, human-escalation line after repeated failures), making the contrast obvious to anyone who hits both. **Users affected:** any participant whose PDF-email request fails. **Remediation:** reuse the same error-copy pattern already established for chat errors — a short, honest line plus a retry affordance — rather than a silent revert.
- The chat-API error state itself (`showAPIError`) is a genuine strength: plain language, a working retry that resends the exact failed payload, and an honest escalation line after two failures ("this is on our end, not yours... email hello@franklyhuman.com"). No finding — noted as the bar the rest of the product should be held to.

### H10 — Help and documentation
- **F9 (Moderate, 2).** No link to a privacy policy, data-handling explanation, or any other documentation exists anywhere in the flow — most relevant on the Intake screen, where a diligence-minded evaluator (Persona 3) is given one confident sentence about data privacy and no way to go deeper. **Users affected:** primarily Persona 3 and any Persona 1/2 user with above-average privacy concern given the corporate-adjacent framing. **Remediation:** a single "read our privacy policy" or "questions about a corporate licence" link on Intake, even if it just routes to email for now.

## 4. Register/tone watch item (not a scored usability finding)

**F12 (Minor, 1).** The Phase‑1 forced-choice buttons and the Phase‑2 trap cards both use a "pick one" visual grammar closely associated with lightweight internet quizzes (two big buttons; three named cards with a one-line description each). The strategy doc explicitly authorizes this exact pattern as a deliberate tradeoff (closer to CliftonStrengths' paired-statement mechanic than a value-chip sort), and the serious typography, required-evidence field (Phase 1), and complete absence of gamified language mostly offset the risk. Flagging only as a design-critic watch item, not a fix-required finding — this is the one place a skeptical eye could momentarily misread the interaction before the copy corrects the impression.
