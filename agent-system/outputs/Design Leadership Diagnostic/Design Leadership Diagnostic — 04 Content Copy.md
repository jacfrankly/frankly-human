## 0. Register note, stated once, load-bearing throughout

This is not the Why Workbook's voice turned up in volume. It's a different register.

The Why Workbook writes to reassure a stranger who's never done this before. This diagnostic writes to a senior practitioner who has done plenty and wants to know if the room across from her has too. That changes specific habits, not just adjectives:

- **No warm-up.** The Why Workbook eases people in. This opens on the first real question's neighbourhood within two screens flat.
- **No permission-seeking.** Never "if you're comfortable" or "whenever you're ready." She's already decided to spend $500 and twenty minutes. Trust that.
- **Precision over softening, everywhere — not just Welcome.** Principle 6 names this for the price and time; it applies to every line in this doc. "This won't be shared with your employer" beats "we take your privacy seriously."
- **Assertions, not questions, in Claude's framing lines.** "Here's what I see" more than "What do you think this means?" — per the curriculum's own tone note: "less 'let's explore this together,' more 'here's what I see, tell me if I'm wrong.'"
- **Grade 6 is still the ceiling on sentence construction** — short sentences, one idea each, plain words. It is not a ceiling on directness or on assuming prior competence. A sentence can be simple and still assume the reader has run a P&L conversation before.
- **No filler acknowledgments.** Cut "Great, thanks for sharing that" and "That makes a lot of sense" — this audience reads warmth-as-padding as a tell that the tool hasn't done the job. Where acknowledgment is needed, it does work (names something specific back) rather than just being polite noise.

Everything below is written against that calibration, phase by phase.

## 2. Intake screen

**Context:** New screen, no Why Workbook equivalent. Sits between Welcome and the diagnostic chat. Function: confirm payment, set expectations for what $500 buys, reassure on privacy, before coaching starts. Per Principle 6, this is not a Stripe-redirect afterthought — it's where the product visibly signals it takes the price seriously.

| Element | Copy |
|---|---|
| Eyebrow | Before you start |
| Title | What this is — and isn't |
| Body — "is" | A direct, structured conversation about where your organisational influence actually sits, and the specific pattern that's keeping it there. Twenty to thirty minutes. One question at a time. A named result at the end, not a follow-up email. |
| Body — "is not" (list) | **It is not a leadership style quiz.** No archetype picked from a menu. **It is not 360-degree feedback.** No one else's opinion of you enters this — only yours, and only about what you've actually seen. **It is not a substitute for executive coaching.** It's a diagnosis, not a program. **It is not Design-Led: Leader.** It's the door to it, not a shorter version of it. |
| Privacy line | What you say here stays with you. If this was purchased through a corporate licence, the person who bought your seat sees that a seat was used — never your answers, never your result. No exceptions. |
| What you leave with (list) | A read on where your real influence sits, across four threads — not a guess, a structured one. The specific leadership trap you're caught in, named. Your Leadership Influence Profile — one sentence built from what you actually said. A direct line from that pattern to what Design-Led: Leader would need to address. One conversation worth having with your own leadership, ready to use this week. |
| Primary CTA | Begin |
| Secondary microcopy under CTA | Takes 20–30 minutes. You can leave and come back. |

**Rationale:**

- The "is / is not" split is lifted directly from the curriculum's own list (per the task brief), tightened to one line each so it reads as a set of flat facts, not a defensive FAQ. "No archetype picked from a menu" and "No one else's opinion of you enters this" translate the curriculum's bullet-point negatives into sentences that tell the reader *why* each thing is excluded, which does more trust-building work than the bare noun phrase alone — without adding warmth, just information.
- The privacy line is written at full confidence per the confirmed business decision: "No exceptions" closes the paragraph as a flat statement, not a policy summary. No "we take privacy seriously," no "rest assured" — those phrases ask to be trusted; "No exceptions" states a fact and lets the reader do the trusting on her own terms, which is the register this audience responds to (see Persona 3, and the Inspiration doc's redaction-bar reference: withholding as the design device, not reassurance-as-performance).
- "What you leave with" upgrades the curriculum's three-lever list to four-thread language implicitly, by never naming a number — it says "four threads" once and lets the rest of the list stay thread-agnostic, so this copy doesn't need editing if a fifth thread is ever added later.
- The secondary CTA microcopy ("You can leave and come back") is the one warm-adjacent line on this screen, and it's there because it's operationally true and relevant to a $500 purchase, not because the tone wants softening — it answers "what if I get interrupted," a real risk at this price point per Section 1.1 of the strategy doc.

## 3. Diagnostic chat — phase-by-phase coaching copy

**Context:** Screen 3. Chat interface, one question at a time, same architecture as the Why Workbook. Speaker differentiation is typographic per the Inspiration doc (mono label, not a coloured bubble) — copy below is written assuming a plain speaker label, no icon, no avatar framing language.

Per Principle 3 (evidence before judgment), every phase requires a real, specific example before Claude moves forward. Per the curriculum's own coaching-flow guardrail, a vague answer is never accepted as final — the follow-up probes below are not optional extras, they're the mechanism that makes Principle 3 real in the actual conversation.

### 3.1 Reframe (pre-phase, no participant input)

Opens the diagnostic. Does not consume a phase slot in the header. No warm-up line before it — this is the first thing Claude says.

> A Head of Design spent eighteen months building a design system the company actually used. Adoption: 90%. Engineering loved it. Then the company set its three-year strategy — and no one thought to ask her into the room.
>
> The work was excellent. It didn't buy her a seat. That's not a rare story — it's the default one, and it has a mechanism, not just a mood.
>
> Let's find yours.

**Rationale:** First two lines near-verbatim from the curriculum draft — already well-crafted, does the "cold open" job the Why Workbook's "5 Mistakes" opener does for the free tool. Added the third line as a bridge into Phase 1 that names the diagnostic's actual claim (a mechanism exists, not just bad luck) without any transition throat-clearing ("Now let's begin" or similar was cut — "Let's find yours" does the job in three words and matches the curriculum's "skip the warm-up" instruction).

### 3.2 Phase 1 — The Four Threads (See)

Phase header: **Phase 1 of 4 · The Four Threads**

Opening line:

> Organisational influence runs through four threads. Most leadership advice only ever talks about the first three — this one doesn't skip the fourth.

Per-thread sequence (repeated four times; the participant sees one thread at a time). Each thread pairs a forced-choice framing (embedded in the thread-scorer component, see Section 4.1) with a follow-up evidence question in the chat:

**Budget Authority**
> Do you shape spend, or execute someone else's call on it?
> Name one recent example — a budget decision you shaped, or one you only heard about after it was made.

**Roadmap Influence**
> Is design setting what's next, or reacting to a roadmap set elsewhere?
> Name one recent example — a roadmap call you made, or one that arrived on your desk already decided.

**Executive Trust**
> Are you in the room when strategy actually gets decided, or briefed on it afterward?
> Name one recent example — a strategy conversation you were in, or one you found out about later.

**Relationship Capital**
> Budget, roadmap, and trust assume the org runs on process. Most don't, not entirely. This one's different: who sponsors you, who owes you, who you owe — and is that map current?
> Name one recent decision that went a certain way because of who was backing it, not because of the argument itself. Were you the one with the backing, or the one without it?

Follow-up if an answer is vague (used at any thread, not just once):

> That's a description, not an example. What's the actual meeting, decision, or moment? Name it.

**Rationale:** The three original threads' evidence prompts stay close to the curriculum's own phrasing ("do you control or meaningfully shape spend"), tightened into a question a participant answers in one line, matching the thread-scorer's forced-choice-plus-evidence mechanic from the strategy doc (Section 1.3). The Relationship Capital prompt is new — it had no curriculum precedent, so it was built from the authoritative doc's own definition ("who sponsors you, who owes you, who you owe, and whether that map is current") and its note on political organisations ("a brilliant business case can lose to a weaker one that has the right sponsor behind it"). The question deliberately doesn't ask "do you have good relationships" (soft, unanswerable, deficit-adjacent) — it asks for one outcome that turned on sponsorship rather than merit, which produces the same evidence-first shape as the other three threads and gives Phase 4 something specific to name if this is the identified thread.

The vague-answer follow-up is written once and reused everywhere a participant under-specifies — this is the interface-level enforcement of Principle 3 and the curriculum's own instruction to "never let a vague answer stand."

### 3.3 Phase 2 — The Three Traps (Sense)

Phase header: **Phase 2 of 4 · The Three Traps**

Opening line:

> Three patterns account for most of what you just described. Which one is yours?

*(Trap selector component appears here — see Section 4.2 for card copy.)*

After selection, probe:

> Is the organisation doing this to you, or did you start doing it to yourself after enough rounds of being overlooked?

Follow-up if the participant answers only "politics" or "culture":

> Politics is the label, not the mechanism. What specifically happened — who decided what, and where were you when it got decided?

**Rationale:** The probe line is near-verbatim from the curriculum draft. The "politics/culture" follow-up operationalises the curriculum's guardrail instruction ("never let 'politics' or 'culture' stand as an unexamined answer") as an actual line Claude says, not just a behavioural note — this is the difference between a guardrail existing in a spec document and existing in the product.

### 3.4 Phase 3 — Where the Room Was Lost (Shape)

Phase header: **Phase 3 of 4 · Where the Room Was Lost**

Three questions, asked one at a time, no softening between them (per the Inspiration doc's Errol Morris reference — the interface's job here is to not visually or verbally rescue the participant from a hard question):

> What's one specific meeting or decision you weren't in, that you should have been?

> What did you do when you found out? What did you want to do?

> What did that cost — the decision's quality, your team's standing, or your own trajectory?

Follow-up if the answer stays general ("I was frustrated," "it wasn't great"):

> Name the cost precisely. A worse decision, a weaker team, or a slower trajectory for you — which one, specifically, and how do you know?

**Rationale:** All three questions near-verbatim from the curriculum. No transition copy inserted between them — per the Inspiration doc's brief for this phase specifically ("no cheerful illustration, no softening colour — just the next question, set exactly like the one before it"), the content-writer's job here is to add nothing, not to add warmth. The follow-up matches the same evidence-forcing pattern as Phase 1 and 2, applied to cost rather than fact.

### 3.5 Phase 4 — The Leadership Influence Profile (Show)

Phase header: **Phase 4 of 4 · The Leadership Influence Profile**

Opening line (before the reveal):

> Here's what I see. Tell me if I'm wrong.

*(Profile reveal card appears — see Section 5 for the sentence itself and example combinations.)*

Line after the reveal, into the bridge:

> That's the pattern. Design-Led: Leader is built to address exactly that gap — not leadership in general, this specific one.

**Rationale:** "Here's what I see. Tell me if I'm wrong" is lifted directly from the curriculum's tone note as a standalone line rather than a description of tone — it's the single clearest embodiment of "less 'let's explore this together,' more 'here's what I see.'" It also does real work: it frames the profile as falsifiable (matching Principle 4 — a diagnosis to act on, not a verdict) while still landing with full authority, no hedge in the sentence itself.

### 3.6 Guardrails (near-verbatim from the curriculum, load-bearing)

These trigger on detection, at any phase, and interrupt the current question sequence.

**Cynicism or defeat** ("nothing will ever change here," or equivalent)
> That might be true of this organisation. It's worth knowing whether it's the organisation or the approach — let's look at one specific moment before we decide which.

**Naming and blaming a specific executive**
> Let's stay with the pattern — what would need to be true structurally for you to be in that room next time?

**Distress or burnout signals**
> That sounds like it's costing you more than a seat at a table. I'm glad you said it. Is there someone you can talk this through with today?

Persistent support line, shown below the message when a distress signal has been flagged (adapted from the Why Workbook's inline resource pattern, register held flat rather than warmed):

> If this is more than a work problem right now: **Lifeline (Australia)** — [13 11 14](tel:131114), free and confidential, 24/7.

**Rationale:** All three guardrail lines are verbatim from the curriculum doc, per the task's explicit instruction — they're already well-crafted and shouldn't be softened or sharpened. The persistent support line is adapted, not copied, from the Why Workbook's `app.js` pattern (`showAPIError`'s distress-flag note): same information, same phone number, but the phrasing drops the Why Workbook's likely softer framing in favour of "more than a work problem" — direct, undramatic, no exclamation, consistent with a tool that has otherwise not used a comforting register anywhere else in the flow. This should render inline, unobtrusive, and only once per session unless distress is flagged again.

## 4. Special component copy

### 4.1 Thread scorer (Phase 1)

One instance per thread (four total), each structurally identical — per the Inspiration doc's Judd-box reference, differentiated only by label and content, never by decoration.

**Component instruction (shown once, above the first thread):**
> For each thread: pick the statement that's true more often than not, then name the example that proves it.

**Per-thread structure (repeated four times):**

| Field | Copy |
|---|---|
| Thread label (mono) | BUDGET AUTHORITY *(then ROADMAP INFLUENCE / EXECUTIVE TRUST / RELATIONSHIP CAPITAL)* |
| Forced-choice option A | I shape this. |
| Forced-choice option B | I execute someone else's call on this. |
| Evidence field label | The example that proves it |
| Evidence field placeholder | *(none — required field, no ghost text; see empty-state copy in Section 6)* |

Thread-specific forced-choice pairs (Budget/Roadmap/Trust use "I shape this" / "I execute someone else's call on this" as the base pattern; Relationship Capital needs its own pair since "shape" doesn't fit a relational construct):

| Thread | Option A | Option B |
|---|---|---|
| Budget Authority | I shape this. | I execute someone else's call on this. |
| Roadmap Influence | I shape this. | I execute someone else's call on this. |
| Executive Trust | I'm in the room when it's decided. | I get briefed after it's decided. |
| Relationship Capital | I know who's backing me, and who I owe. | I don't have a current picture of who's actually behind me. |

**Rationale:** Kept "I shape this / I execute someone else's call" as the base pattern for Budget and Roadmap (matches the strategy doc's own suggested framing verbatim), but built distinct pairs for Executive Trust and Relationship Capital rather than forcing all four threads into one sentence template — "shape" doesn't honestly describe trust or sponsorship, and stretching the metaphor would cost precision for the sake of consistency, which this product can't afford (Principle 3's evidence-first spirit extends to not letting the UI's own copy get abstract). All four pairs stay first-person, present tense, and roughly matched in length so the four cards read as a genuine set, not four different registers.

### 4.2 Trap selector (Phase 2)

Three cards, tap to select one as "feels most true." One-line description each, pulled and tightened from the curriculum's own trap definitions.

| Trap | Card copy |
|---|---|
| The Service Department Trap | Brought in to execute a decision someone else already made. |
| The Feature Factory Manager | Measured on velocity and output — never on outcomes or strategy. |
| The Silent Expert | Has the strategic opinion. Has learned not to offer it unless asked. |

**Rationale:** Each line stays under 12 words, matches the curriculum's own phrasing closely enough to be recognisable if a participant has read anything about the program before, and — per Principle 4 — describes a system response ("brought in to," "measured on," "has learned not to") rather than a personal failing. None of the three implies the participant chose this; each implies the pattern was built around her.

Sentence 4 covers Relationship Capital, the added fourth thread — deliberately paired with Silent Expert rather than Service Department Trap, because a sponsorship gap and a silenced opinion compound each other in a way that's specific to political organisations, per the authoritative curriculum's own note on this thread. Every sentence passes the "would she say this out loud" test from Principle 2 — none of them sound like a report; they sound like something a sharp colleague would say back to her over coffee.

## 6. Error and empty states

Register note: these are not the Why Workbook's error copy warmed down or up — they're built fresh, at the same directness as the rest of this doc. Same underlying principle (tell them what happened, tell them what to do, no blame) but no cushioning language ("oops," "no worries," "all good").

### 6.1 API / connection errors

| State | Copy |
|---|---|
| Timeout | This is taking longer than it should. |
| General failure | That didn't go through. |
| Retry button | Try again |
| Persistent failure (2+ retries) | Still not going through — this is on our end, not yours. If it keeps happening, email hello@franklyhuman.com and I'll pick up the conversation from where you left off. |

**Rationale:** Matches `app.js`'s existing pattern of a short diagnosis line plus a retry button, but drops "Something went wrong" entirely — content-writer.md rules this out explicitly, and it's doubly wrong here: vague fault-location reads as evasive to an audience this senior. "This is on our end, not yours" does the opposite — names fault plainly, which this audience will trust more than a hedge.

### 6.2 Payment / intake errors

| State | Copy |
|---|---|
| Payment not confirmed on Intake screen load | We don't have a confirmed payment for this session yet. If you just paid, give it a moment and refresh — if it still doesn't show, email hello@franklyhuman.com with your receipt. |
| Session expired mid-diagnostic | Your session timed out. Your answers up to Phase {n} are saved — pick up where you left off. |

**Rationale:** "We don't have a confirmed payment" states the actual system state rather than a generic "access denied" — at $500, a participant who has genuinely paid needs to know immediately that this is a sync delay, not a rejection of her purchase. The session-expiry copy names the specific phase reached, matching Principle 3's evidence-first spirit even in an error state — precision instead of "your progress was saved."

### 6.3 Form / input validation

| State | Copy |
|---|---|
| Empty name field on Welcome | Your name goes here — it's how I'll address you through this. |
| Invalid email on close screen | That's not a valid email address. |
| Thread-scorer evidence field left empty | Every thread needs one real example. Pick the moment, then name it. |
| Trap selector — no card selected | Pick the one that's most true right now — not the one you wish were true. |

**Rationale:** The thread-scorer and trap-selector validation copy is the sharpest test of the "no blame, but no softening either" balance: "Pick the one that's most true right now — not the one you wish were true" isn't blame (it doesn't say the participant did anything wrong), but it also doesn't apologise for enforcing Principle 3 — it states the standard the diagnostic is holding, which is itself in-register: this product has already told her, on the Intake screen, that evidence comes before judgment. The validation message should feel like the tool keeping a promise it already made, not a new rule appearing.
